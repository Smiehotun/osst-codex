I

How the game works

Before the story, it helps to understand the stage: the Warehouse, four worlds at the cardinal points, the three tiers of the Overworld, the “stars” that lead into pocket worlds, and the ladder of ranks the Visitor climbs.

Four worlds and where they come from

The game takes place in a Warehouse: an artificial world that the Order of the Sinking Star assembled from fragments of four other worlds. Each world comes from a real free puzzle game by another designer, redrawn and built into a shared map. The game names the Warehouse outright: the sign sign_facility announces “THIS WAREHOUSE IS A 4-COMBINATOR” and advises you to “take appropriate measures.”

The directions are confirmed three times. In the final scene, Magistrate Reynard calls the heroes in exactly this way: “North!” for the heroes, “East!” for the Trader, “West!” for the Caver, “South!” for the Sailor (coda). Leamington advises going North and to “avoid the West until you are ready for sterner work” (sokoban_overworld). The Acting Director calls the North and West worlds “quite bold”: they “import a large number of objects and behaviors” (audiolog_sky_why_so_big_3). The WikiTactics fan map lays out the zones the same way.

Three of the four source games were made in PuzzleScript, the open-source puzzle engine that Stephen Lavelle (author of Stephen’s Sausage Roll) released in 2013. His name isn’t in the game’s credits; the critic Matthewmatosis (chapter XII) calls him the unsung hero of the whole story.

The worlds’ rules in brief (after the WikiTactics fan guide)
  • The Warrior pushes any row of objects, stacks included, and can bear any load. The Thief doesn’t push: with every step she pulls along whatever stood behind her. The Wizard doesn’t push either; he swaps places with the first hero, block, monster or gem in his line of sight. The Priestess protects herself and her neighbors (including those above and below) from goblins and dragon fire. Everything within two tiles of the Bard repeats her steps. The Druid, when he runs into an obstacle, transforms it: crystals and trees swap forms, and everything else becomes a crystal.
  • Goblins kill unprotected neighbors after every move. Dragons don’t move; their fire travels to the first obstacle, and gems and trees stop it. Gates open when every button of their color is pressed; a level is solved when a hero stands on every exit.
  • Mirrors reflect along two perpendicular axes and carry all reflections over at once; two mirrors on one object make a clone, and reflections that coincide merge.
  • Skipping stones live in real time: a stone skips across water until it reaches land or a lily pad; a stone that gets stuck sinks and becomes a bridge; lily pads drift with the current.
  • Beams pass through walls, but closed gates, gems, boulders, other emitters and the backs of mirrors stop them. An emitter shines only when every gem socket in the room is filled. Colors are properties: Open opens gates, Through takes you through walls, Smash turns a block into rubble, Double doubles your step, Strong lets you push like the Warrior, Endpoint carries you to the end of the beam, Squeeze lets you pass through fences, Alter changes what the other colors do: with it Double triples your step, Endpoint carries you to the middle of the beam, and Strong becomes the power to pull code The rarest color is Gem: whatever stands in such a beam powers the sockets next to it, as a gem would; on the Overworld, all such emitters are down below. And one edge case is all but lethal: a beam that runs out of the level with nothing to stop it carries whoever steps along it off the map, and only undo brings them back (chapter III).
  • Artifacts of the West: the torc turns every beam that hits you toward the direction you face; the rod teleports the object in front of you to the next free tile, jumping walls and gates; the prism switches the emitter in front of you to the next color in its set.

A diagram of combinations: four points and six edges

The game’s governing device is combinatorial: it brings the four worlds together first in pairs, then in threes, and finally all four at once. Draw the four worlds as points and join every pair with a line, and you get a square with both diagonals: the complete graph on four vertices. This is exactly the figure that the first ending ceremonially “unfreezes,” dimension by dimension (chapter VIII), and exactly what the sign with the Order’s emblem describes: “nodes to represent the Worlds, and segments for the connections between the Worlds” (sign_new_star).

This is a diagram of combinations, not a map of the terrain: on the map, hybrid zones usually sit inside the region of one of the worlds (almost all combinations with the heroes are in the North). Zones are assigned to edges by their names and by the levels inside them; the four whose names don’t reveal a pair are listed separately in the center card. Every one of the 83 zones has a place inference

Why whole worlds, not a bag of mechanics

Blow has explained the device himself, in a talk in China in July 2026. Designers sometimes draw a game as a matrix of its objects, one cell for every pair that interacts; the number of cells grows with the square of the objects, and his slide adds a moral: “density is good.” But the same picture shows the trap. Add objects one at a time and “eventually it feels flat due to loss of structure”: each new thing blurs into the last. Worlds are his way out. Each source game arrives whole, already dense and already fun, so when two collide every object of one can meet every object of the other, and yet, as another slide puts it, “it’s not this messy in your mind; the structure of the 4 games helps organize.” The Warrior, the Thief and the Wizard belong together in the player’s head; so do stones and lily pads. The first plan had six games; the four that shipped were always at the top of the list, and none was ever swapped out. The Order tells its own history the same way: its earliest Warehouses mixed single objects until “the outcomes tended towards inscrutability,” and only then did it start combining whole worlds (chapter VII). One difference matters. On Blow’s slide density is good; in the Order’s physics density is what rots the Warehouses (chapter VII).

The three tiers of the Overworld

Internal zone IDs begin with one of three letters: area_g_, area_s_ and area_u_. These are tiers: the Antipode exists on the ground, up high (Antipode High, s) and down low (Antipode Low, u), and Crossroads and the Exit work the same way. g is the ground, s is the upper tier (the same letter marks the audiolog_sky_* recordings), u is the underground inference The code confirms it: there the tiers are literally called GROUND, SKY and UNDERWORLD code

The tiers differ in meaning. The ground holds the four worlds and all the pairs. Up high the game becomes three-dimensional: vertical mirrors, falling, the “Top of the World.” Down below lie spaces that wrap around onto themselves (Wrapping Mirrors, Wrapping Wizard, Endless Skipping), “Beam Computation,” and the zones “Three at Once” and “Four Together.” Even the ground floor wraps: a stand with drafts of the map labels the final version a “wrapping ground floor” and notes that the wrapping let the gold rooms be placed symmetrically (sign_overworld_design_3). Hence the “Antipode”: on a closed surface, it is the point opposite the center inference The code confirms this as well: the Overworld is a torus of 190 × 156 squares, and the Antipode hubs sit exactly half a period from the Crossroads on both axes, at the point farthest from it code There is an Antipode on every tier, and every tier has its own ending; on Steam they are called just that: Endgame, Endgame High and Endgame Low (chapter VIII).

The voices are sorted by tier as well. Leamington’s beacons (audiolog_g_*) play on the ground. The Acting Director’s talks about meaning, history and the “Play of Form” are up high. The Director’s conversations about decay, chaos and computation are below, next to “Beam Computation.” The result is an almost theological vertical: foundations and decay in the depths, the pilgrimage in the middle, meaning overhead inference Within the tiers, too, recordings seem to stand next to puzzles on the same subject (chapter XII).

The tier comes from the internal ID; the region and puzzle counts come from the WikiTactics fan map, which lists 920 puzzles in 78 zones. That is every star on the Overworld: the code counts the same number, and the Completionist achievement asks for all of them code The “more than 1,400” is a different count. Blow has explained that it measures puzzles, not levels, so it also takes in the Overworld’s own puzzles and the insides of the bigger levels; by his latest estimate it is “probably around 1,500.” He also refuses to give players a target number, since then “the game just becomes about grinding until you hit the target number” (Wookash Podcast, October 2026). This codex gives you one anyway: treat it as a map, not a quota. The localization file names 83 zones; five of them are not on the fan map: the ground-level Crossroads, the Skipping Way, the Beam Way, Antipode Low and a mysterious zone called “Beamcaster” (more on it below). The first four simply have no puzzles of their own: they are hubs and roads.

Internal names that give the design away

  • area_u_zachtronics is “Beam Computation.” A nod to Zachtronics, the studio known for programming puzzles: here, beams work like logic circuits. The zone’s four levels are named accordingly: zachtronics_identity, zachtronics_or, zachtronics_xor and plain zachtronics: identity, OR, XOR code
  • area_s_porphyry is “Vertical Endpoints.” Porphyry is a purple stone, and the Endpoint beam glows purple: the commentator in the revisions hall calls it “the far endpoint of the purple Beam.” Purple is also the Bard’s color: according to the director’s note she wears a purple outfit and admires this light (hp_bard_purple), and in the final scene she travels by an endpoint beam inference
  • area_s_startle_reflex is “Goblin Turning”: the goblins’ startle reflex. According to the critic, the recording about predictability and surprise lies right here (chapter XII).
  • area_u_lemonade is “Mirrorable Mirror Medley,” and it takes its name from one of its own levels, lemonade code Why lemonade? If these levels really build cellular automata, the old saying gets a second bottom: “when life gives you lemons,” and “Life” is Conway’s automaton (chapter XII) hypothesis
  • The West’s zones are called area_g_promise_* internally, and the achievement for its campaign is PROMISE in the internal data (“Beamcasters” on Steam). Blow has confirmed the trace: during development the world went by The Promise, and it was renamed for release (Wookash Podcast).
  • area_g_beamcaster is “Beamcaster,” plain and simple. The name isn’t in the zone list at the top of the file but at its very end, between the signs and the error messages, next to area_g_promise_1 (“Vault of the Beamcasters”), which is likewise listed only there. No zone on the map uses this name: every other name has its own pin on the Overworld, this one has none code It looks like the trace of a renaming, or of a zone added or cut at the last minute hypothesis

Stars, pockets, cages, gold rooms and towers

  • Star: the entrance to a single puzzle. Leamington calls the stars “the entries to other worlds” and notices that they “decrease in density” farther from the center (audiolog_g_no_eating).
  • Pocket: the puzzle itself. The menu item reads “Exit this Pocket” (menu_exit_level); on the Overworld, restarting is not allowed (no_restart_on_the_overworld).
  • Silvery cages block the way “until”: until you know what they want you to know. Leamington realizes that each cage is “invisibly joined to its star, each star holding an idea,” and that together they would trace “a delicate webwork,” a chart of relations “in that realm of Ideas” (audiolog_g_musing_on_freezers_1). Their internal name is “freezers,” and in the first ending the command “unfreeze” literally thaws the next block.
  • Gold rooms (Leamington’s “gilded decks”): gilded platforms with a star in the middle, six of them at the Antipode. Leamington finds them one by one and sees their layout as mathematical, though “you might choose to see it as ceremonial” (audiolog_g_how_many_gold_rooms). For builders who can conjure whole worlds, gold “could scarcely be precious,” so the display is probably meant “to dazzle the uninitiated” (audiolog_g_gold_rooms).
  • Golden platforms inside the worlds look the same but do a different job. In the code they are Overworld_Unlock, and they stand in the five long story levels of the West and the South code Touching one marks the whole level as partly solved, and that single flag thaws particular cages back on the Overworld, so the map opens up before the long level is finished. That is the “great pulse” Janaila sends “toward some other place”: “A message, perhaps. A short one. What it said? No idea” (skipping_stones_shrine). Short indeed: one bit. The Caver steps onto one with “This place again...” (promesst2_gold_room), and in the first mine he feels the same signal leave: “I feel something important just happened, but ... no idea what” (promesst1_unlock).
  • Towers: progress counters code There are six on the ground, one for each pair of worlds, four up high, one per world, and six more pairs below. A tower activates once enough of its levels are solved (6 to 12 on the ground and up high), and those levels are marked by sixteen-pointed stars instead of the usual eight-pointed ones. An active tower switches on a teleporter tied to the Antipode of its tier. On the ground it leads straight to one of the gold rooms (on Steam this earns achievements named like “Antipode HxM”), and four open gold rooms unlock the first ending; the four sky towers open the way to the second.
  • Map rooms: three miniature copies of the whole world, ground, high and low, hung above the sky. The world’s major teleporters lead into the map room of their tier, and there each one has a twin: stepping on it takes you to the original. The copies fill in as the Visitor discovers teleporters code That is how fast travel works: to reach the far side of the world, you walk across its model.
  • The Lobby: the hall in front of the Warehouse, decorated in “Lobby Theme 1a,” a tribute to the school that inspired the Order’s founder (more in chapter VII). Each of the three endings brings the Queen back here.

The ladder of ranks

The game grants the Visitor ranks (“Rank Granted,” rank_granted). There are six, handed out like this code: the odd ones for the three endings, the even ones for first reaching the upper and lower tiers. A rank can only go up, and it opens nothing by itself: it lets certain Signs, once read, thaw the cages tied to them.

Who tells the story

Count every word in the subtitles and the picture is unexpected. Half the text belongs to the Order and the Overworld: the Acting Director, the Director and Leamington. The Queen herself, through whose eyes we play, gets just over one percent, and the Priestess gets eight ellipses.

II

North: The Hearty Heroes of Hauling

The game’s longest storyline: movers who meant to haul crystals out of a manor and turned out to be pieces in someone else’s production.

North, the world of The Hearty Heroes of Hauling

A world without great waters

Leamington notes that two of the Warehouse’s worlds have water “so dense it will drown you at the mere depth of a man,” while in the others water “repels itself when agglomerated beyond a certain size,” so that “there’s no such thing as an expanse of water” (audiolog_g_world_selection_1). The Heroes’ world is one of the latter. A director’s note specifies that it has no water-dwelling creatures of any kind, which is why nobody there knows what an alligator is (heroes3_victory_lap). At the shore, Valens can’t believe “this is what water is like when it all just ... stays together” (hw_first), and Tif asks Janaila how she managed to gather so much water in one place.

Society here is a kingdom, with a head Magistrate over its courts. West and East are divided by the Badlands. They are crossed by the Crawler, a huge overland transport with a passenger terminal (the “passengers’ retrenchment”), and once a season by the Royal Caravan, whose defenses are legendary. Beyond the canyon lies the East: mountains with goblin caves, the Bard’s homeland, and the Circle of Elders that the Druid serves.

The company

Valens, Tif and Isidore started a moving company, The Hearty Heroes of Hauling. Isidore lobbied for “hale” instead (“‘Hale Heroes of Hauling’ sounds upscale and smart”), but the others didn’t like the word (heroes2_wizard_goblins_mid). All three had experience at loading docks, and Valens and Tif worked at the western end of the Crawler’s route until Tif, as a note puts it, “stole something very tantalizing from the purser’s quarters” (heroes2_thief_priestess_crawler). The joke is right on the surface: in a sokoban game, a game about a “warehouse keeper,” the main heroes are movers.

The story step by step

  1. The appraisal. Magistrate Reynard hires the company to estimate the cost of moving the crystals out of his manor. At a banquet he had described the job to Valens as much smaller than it really was (heroes1_warrior_wizard_confused). During the appraisal Tif walks off with the Magistrate’s “fine leather-clad case, with satisfyingly grippable handles”: she hoped it was full of shiny things, but “it only held papers and notes” (heroes_three_for_the_first_time_mandatory_end).
  2. The dungeon. When the heroes give their estimate, Reynard has them jailed. Then he opens Valens’s cage himself: “Watch, Ratleigh, as we free the mouse, and he skitters through our maze” (heroes_mouse). Isidore never understands how he got out: “some kind of intervention from afar” (heroes1_wizard_1).
  3. The notes. Tif admits she read the papers. They reveal how the crystals all around are made: they are transmuted people (a note to heroes_three_for_the_first_time_optional). Even before her confession she speaks to the crystals as if they were alive: “Whoever you are, hold tight. We’ll pull you out of this” (heroes1_thief_three_crystals_end).
  4. The Crawler key. Tif noticed that Reynard had already “donned his dustcoat. For travel,” and that around his neck hung a plaited leather cord with a key to a locker on the Crawler: so, the East (heroes1_warrior_thief_mid). The second trap has no bars, and comes with a jibe at the “Gravy Sorcerer” (heroes_jail2). The Crawler can’t be caught, which leaves the Royal Caravan, whose season starts in two weeks. The heroes decide to “play it up a little, insinuate he’s looking to usurp the throne” (heroes_jail2_mid2). The King charges them with the capture and promises to have Valens’s Swordbinding dispelled (heroes2_warrior_3monster).
  5. The Caravan and the monsters. The Caravan is broken up, a town is overrun by goblins, the heroes are scattered. Tif suspects Reynard: “Such an attack has hardly ever happened in history” (heroes2_three_first). The silent Priestess joins the group. Isidore talks through the possibilities: a coincidence; Reynard knew the invasion was coming; Reynard caused it (heroes2_wizard_per_turn_goblins). From the shadows, Reynard’s voice praises “the complexities of this mechanism” that slow the heroes down, and since walls did not contain them, he puts dragons “to this use” (heroes2_thief_wizard_priestess_compartments, heroes2_four).
  6. Beyond the canyon. In the East the Bard and the Druid join them. The Druid was sent by the Circle of Elders, because Reynard stole the secret of transmutation (heroes3_warrior_and_druid_transmutation). On his father’s orders, Ratleigh has cleared the area of anything Isidore could use “to weasel past this faithful Guard Dragon” (heroes3_four_with_dragon).
  7. The victory lap. The six stand at a “color fringe,” beyond which lies a “vast” unknown land. Reynard tells his story, names ten “elements” (each one flashes on screen), snaps his fingers (“the act is consummated”) and leaves with “See you later, Alligators!” (heroes3_victory_lap). The Druid sums up: “He flees, we follow, and apprehend. Simple.” (heroes3_victory_lap_end).

Reynard’s confession

The monologue at the end of the storyline runs almost four minutes, and it is a key to the whole game. Some months earlier, Reynard was kneeling by his bed, praying out loud (“so that God sees I really mean it, you know”). Two things tormented him: what to do with his son Ratleigh, “such a useless lump,” and whether his own “time of youthful creativity” was “expended.” He had been successful: he “greatly increased the efficiency of the prisons” and stopped criminals from feeding “upon the upright citizenry,” but over the years it had all become “paperwork, maintenance,” a slate of convictions “that really anyone could prosecute” in his stead.

Then a woman appeared before him, “bathed in multicolored light, beatific.” She “was not an angel, but she might as well have been.” She had “pledged her life to walking in the ways of God,” and she asked for his help: to bring a Warrior, a Thief, a Wizard, a Priestess, a Bard, a Druid, Goblins, Fire-breathing Dragons, Crystals, Buttons and Energy Gates together in one place. It “was very difficult to eventuate, and took lavish expenditure.” She told him he had “truly exceptional powers of Judgement” and promised “a new way to sophisticate my Judgement, in and among a wider Existence.” “To be seen so clearly released in me a torrent of love.”

But you haven’t failed, because now we’re all a part of something bigger.

Reynard, heroes3_victory_lap

The game makes clear who the woman is without naming her: the Order’s Acting Director inference A director’s note says that as Reynard describes her, he is aware she may be watching him. Later the Acting Director happily repeats his phrase about “lavish expenditure,” and a note explains that she heard it from the Magistrate and finds it funny (audiolog_sky_why_so_big_3). She also says that she built this Warehouse herself, and calls its northern world one of the “bold” ones for the sheer number of elements it imports.

The cast

Blow built these characters from their mechanics outward. Each hero comes with a useful skill and with “some kind of psychology that prevents them from being measured and moderate in their use of their abilities”: “everyone in this world is actually a little bit pathological” (livestream with Edmund McMillen, October 2026). Read the cards with that in mind: each gift is also the shape of a flaw (chapter X).

Valens, the Warrior

warrior · voiced by Liam O’Brien

A former swordsman under a Swordbinding: he can’t swing anything at anyone, can’t even punch a goblin (“I could push them sternly with my fist”). What he was punished for is never said outright: the higher-ups only pretend to hate him, and the rank and file respect him (one reading is below). His enormous pushing strength comes from a technique taught by another Swordbound soldier: personal energy squeezed through the restriction is amplified (a note to spin_doctors_end). That teacher later turns up in the Warehouse itself, and asked how he got there, says: “I just ... came in!” (spin_doctors). Lonely, dreams of a family and grandkids; his favorite exclamation is “Oh my God.”

Tif, the Thief

thief · voiced by Sabrina Fest

Can only pull, and can’t stop: there is no pull button, she drags whatever stood behind her with every step, and can pull herself into a dead end. Cheerful, chatty, friends with everyone, dragons included (“I don’t suppose anyone ever keeps you as pets?”). Blow reads her character straight off the rule: “her weakness is that she brings too many things too close to her”; she “collects friends,” calls everybody friend, “and she even likes the bad guy.” In the coda she calls Reynard “a ‘friend’ of mine,” and the note asks for heavy sarcasm: she knows exactly who he is, and the word still comes out. She is the one who read Reynard’s notes and first understood that the crystals are alive. The only one who knows the Priestess’s real name. Even Aiah’s effect works differently on her: around the Priestess others confess their inner troubles, while Tif talks about her friends (a note to heroes2_thief_priestess_second). She feels watched: “Someone creepy cares about me...” (heroes1_5). Plans to bring gourds of this strange ‘water’ home on a big cart and sell it to the idle rich: “Foreign, mysterious ... maybe dangerous... it sells itself!” Valens reasonably worries the water won’t stay stuck together back home, leaving him “next to a cartload of ... exploding water pellets” (hw_warrior_thief).

Isidore (“Izzy”), the Wizard

wizard · voiced by Darin De Paul

Swaps places with things. For ten years he was obsessed with how to teleport something “bigger than a ... a quince.” At 3 in the morning, deciding he’d had “enough careful,” he dropped a stone “shimmering with power”; tiny fragments lodged in his body for good (heroes2_wizard_and_priestess_alone_third). “Gravy Sorcerer”: a note says he really did research how to keep gravy warm on the table, “with MODERATE SUCCESS mind you.” His swap powers are “great for moving heavy stuff, not so great for getting close to people.” He once ruined a candlelit evening with the word “donkey,” though, he assures the Priestess, he admired “the sheen of her hairy, hairy legs”: “nice hair, I mean, soft and lustrous” (heroes2_wizard_and_priestess_alone_first).

Aiah, the Priestess

priestess · no voice

The “Golden Girl” who shields her neighbors from monsters. The credits: “The Priestess has taken a vow of silence.” She crosses water with ease, as if floating over it, and Tif is fondly jealous: “when I see you just float across... I’m inspired” (vm_thief_priestess_3_mid). Around her, everyone starts confessing, everyone but Tif. The Druid says his people have “legends of her kind”; a note adds that he won’t share them because they are secret, and disturbing. Later he gives something away with a single word (below). Only Tif knows her name; she makes her way to her across the “big water” and rescues her from an old goblin fortress (vm_thief_priestess_*).

The Bard

bard · voiced by Anjali Bhimani

Every living thing near her repeats her steps; so do the crystals, because “they are filled with life.” Rumors of those crystals are what brought her East, and once she has seen them she sets herself a goal: “Now, to find the man who’s making this happen” (heroes3_bard_crystals_first_end). She tells the Priestess how she learned to catch “glimpses”: “This person wants to be reached in this way. That person, that way.” And “now ... this is just how I live” (heroes3_bard_and_priestess_mid). You can hear it in her speech: to the Warrior she talks about moving “forward,” with the Druid she borrows his word “handle,” and the notes point out that this is her way of meeting each person on his own ground (heroes3_bard_warrior_first, heroes3_bard_and_druid_second). She remembers her three years in the West as “such a happy time,” when she “was just learning to be an adult, to interact with all these strangers” (hp_bard_town_mid2). What is hidden here

The crystals we push are people

Tif says “whoever you are”; Isidore finds them “strangely cut,” “lifelike, almost ... possessed” (heroes1_wizard_array_end); the Bard confirms “they are filled with life”; and the heroes’ pitch to the King rests on the Magistrate “crystalizing lesser nobles without rhyme nor reason.” This makes the mechanics darker inference The crystals that fill Reynard’s levels, the blocks the player uses to hold down buttons and crush goblins, are enchanted people; only the Druid’s own crystals, made from trees and monsters, are something else. Valens says “Thank God they didn’t foresee I’d push crystals as a weapon” (heroes2_warrior_monster_gate_2_optional_end), and Isidore is glad the crystals were never hauled away: “I’d have naught to work with now.”

And “without rhyme nor reason” is true only from the heroes’ side. A director’s note says Reynard is transmuting people into crystals “for an unknown reason” (heroes_three_for_the_first_time_optional), yet in his confession “Crystals” are on the list of the very ten elements he was asked to bring together in one place. And in Heroes 2 Tif finds a “fresh” crystal: “Magistrate Reynard has been here, and not long ago” (heroes2_thief_dragons_second); the transmutations go on during the chase itself. It seems people were turned into blocks for the Warehouse’s future puzzles, and the “lavish expenditure” here was paid in other people’s lives inference Against this background, the judge’s words in the third ending ring especially clear: “We hold a high position. We must not use it for cruelty.”

Telling names

Reynard is the name of the fox in the medieval Reynard the Fox, a trickster tried at the king’s court. Here the fox sits on the bench himself, as the King’s chief magistrate; he names his son Ratleigh and watches the hero skitter like “the mouse” through “our maze.” A fox, a rat for an heir, a mouse for a pastime. Valens is Latin for “strong” inference

Aiah and a word too apt

The Druid is wary of her: “I’ll say only that I am uneasy” (heroes3_druid_and_priestess_1). When Aiah is held at an old goblin fortress, he, as Tif tells it, “insisted he couldn’t do anything about it,” though “He’s the only guy who can petrify these jerks!” (vm_thief_priestess_1). And yet at one point he admits: “I’m warming up to her. She’s been a consistent ... godsend.” A note says that as he says “godsend,” he “realizes he’s a bit too on the nose” (heroes3_four_with_button_quad). Since his people’s secret legends are about “her kind,” the word reads like a slip: those legends seem to be about beings sent from above inference He goes on, “Quite pretty as well,” and the Bard teases at once: “Prettier than me?”

The fortress scene itself is written to the letter of the rules. The stage direction for Tif’s arrival: she sees “the Priestess suspended high in the air in an unlikely manner, surrounded by a bunch of goblins” (vm_thief_priestess_6). In the game’s rules, protection lifts no one by itself. The Priestess and anyone beside her are merely “ready” (pre_protected); one of them becomes “protected,” held in place over water or empty air, only in the turn when a goblin’s blow or a dragon’s fire actually lands, and when the attack stops, the hold clears and they fall or drown code For the Priestess herself the rule is plain; for her neighbors the teardown still lists a couple of recorded oddities, so its edges are not fully mapped. So Aiah hangs in the air because the goblins keep hitting her. The designers even call it flying: two levels in Heroes and Water are named cleric_fly_intro3_v2 and new_cleric_fly, and cleric is the Priestess’s internal name. Tif’s fond “when I see you just float across” describes the same rule from inside the story inference

hypothesisWhy the Druid “couldn’t do anything”

Tif can’t believe it: he is “the only guy who can petrify these jerks!” But that is exactly the problem. The Druid’s power is to turn monsters into crystals, and a crystal strikes no one. Petrify the goblins and the blows stop; stop the blows, and the woman they hold aloft drops. Over dry ground that is only a fall. But the fortress stands across the “big water,” and if she hangs over it, the fall is a drowning: protection does not stop drowning, and once the hold clears, a hero over water sinks. His refusal may be neither cowardice nor distrust but the one correct reading of the rules: the obvious rescue could kill her. Tif goes for her herself.

The rules make her a saint in one more way. Of the six heroes, the Priestess is the only one whose skill moves nothing and transforms nothing: the Warrior pushes, the Thief pulls, the Wizard swaps, the Bard leads, the Druid transforms, and she only keeps others alive. When the Queen puts on her outfit at a wardrobe, that skill even replaces the Queen’s basic push, and a single object resting on top crushes her code In the mechanics the holy woman touches no one, bears no weight, and rises only while evil is striking her inference

hypothesisA biblical name

Aiah is a biblical name, and a man’s: its best-known bearer is the father of Rizpah (2 Samuel 3:7). Rizpah is remembered for one thing. From the beginning of harvest until the rains she kept watch over the bodies of her executed sons and “suffered neither the birds of the air to rest on them by day, nor the beasts of the field by night” (2 Samuel 21:10), doing what the Priestess does in the mechanics: shielding the defenseless from predators. And the Hebrew ayyah is a keen-sighted bird of prey, a kite or falcon; it is the word behind the path “which the vulture’s eye hath not seen” (Job 28:7). A silent guardian who floats over the water: it fits almost too well, but the game confirms none of it.

hypothesisHiring the movers was part of the plan

Reynard needed a Warrior, a Thief and a Wizard, and exactly these three make up the company. He described the job as smaller than it was, jailed the heroes, then let them loose into “our maze” himself. He may not have planned the theft of the case, but it gave the heroes a reason to chase him East, where the Bard, the Druid and the Priestess were waiting, and so it brought all the “elements” together in one place. The others were drawn in by the same thing: the Druid by the stolen technique of transmutation, the Bard by rumors of living crystals. The crystals seem to have served not only as blocks but as bait.

hypothesisThe dragons were brought from the West

The Bard calls one a “Western fire lizard” that her “new friends” told her to be “quite wary” of (heroes3_bard_dragon_maze). Tif, on the other hand, is delighted: “They remind me of home!” (hw_warrior_thief_dragons). Guardsmen saw “incredible smoke” coming from the mountains, and Isidore wonders whether someone smoked the goblins out of their caves, while dragons “don’t mind smoke.” If the dragons are western, Reynard most likely brought them, which would explain where the invasion of the East came from.

hypothesisWho makes the crystals in the East

Tif describes Reynard as “Rich. Manicured... Theatrical,” in the garb “of the highest station.” The Bard replies: “Strange. That’s not the description I’ve heard” (heroes3_bard_thief_mid). Perhaps Reynard traveled in disguise. This echoes the stranger who talked the Trader into sailing to the Mirror Isles: dressed as a commoner, though “everything he wore was oddly crisp,” and speaking of his refined “judgement” (chapter V).

Why Valens is bound

The game never says outright, but it leaves three clues. The higher-ups only “pretend to hate” him, while “the regular guys on the line, they respect what I did”; as Tif puts it, “They all wanted to be him, ... and they were glad not to be him” (heroes2_three_first_mid). She asks the Priestess not to repeat her word “disgraced”: “They have some official term for it. A neutral term” (heroes2_thief_priestess_second_mid_disgraced). And Valens’s teacher, by Tif’s account “another disgraced soldier” (heroes2_thief_priestess_second_mid_warrior), greets him with “We Swordbound must stand together!” (spin_doctors_end). So there are enough of the punished to make a brotherhood inference

hypothesisInsubordination for a just cause

Taken together, it sounds like a deed that command is obliged to condemn and the ranks can’t help respecting: refusing an order, or raising a sword against his own commander, for the sake of the men in the line. The punishment strikes exactly where the crime was, at the sword arm, and the “neutral term” lets no one call him either a hero or a traitor.

Where Reynard got the technique

The Druid is impressed that a stranger could learn a method that “normally requires years of training” (heroes3_warrior_and_druid_transmutation). Back in Heroes 2, Isidore recalls hearing tell “of exotic women who can enthrall monsters and men”; Tif wonders whether Reynard has one in his service, and Isidore suggests he “absorbed that power himself, as he did with that ... crystallization.” Reynard, listening from the shadows, answers for himself: “Wouldn’t you like to know!” (heroes2_thief_wizard_priestess_compartments_mid). A chapter later the group is joined by the Bard, who does exactly that inference Who taught the Magistrate, the game doesn’t say. His own words narrow it down, though: it all began “just some months ago actually,” and both the Crystals and the Druid are on the list of elements the woman in multicolored light asked him to assemble. A technique the druids teach for years, mastered in months by a man the Order had just recruited, points to the Order hypothesis

The Druid’s words that explain the mechanics

The world was once “a pile of rocks,” from which life slowly grew; every “mote of flesh” still holds “the full course of our slow transformation from lithic quietude to animal messiness,” and to be transmuted is “a sort of ... descent through corporeal memory” (heroes3_wizard_and_druid_2). He tells Janaila he can’t choose the form: the transmuted “retain their spirit, and I realign form to spirit” (hs_sailor_druid_1). The mechanics follow this almost to the letter code A crystal made from a creature or an object stores its type, and a hero’s crystal also stores his skills and items, so the Druid’s next bump brings exactly that back. But a crystal that sat in the level from the start has no past: it becomes a tree, one of five at random. So within the game’s rules, the people turned into crystals before the heroes arrived cannot be brought back: the Druid can only make trees of them. The story never says what becomes of them. The mechanics answer quietly, and Tif’s “We’ll pull you out of this” is a promise they don’t keep inference

Loose threads

  • The promised pardon is never shown: in the coda, Reynard is the one giving the heroes orders, and Tif calls him, with heavy sarcasm, “a ‘friend’ of mine.”
  • The Druid’s mission, to bring Reynard before the Circle, also stays unfinished.
  • Ratleigh does not follow his father: the “special transport” was one “beyond which his son was not to follow” (heroes3_bard_wizard_druid_pondering). Since his father was grooming him “to assume my duties here,” Ratleigh apparently stays on as Magistrate inference
  • The storyline’s last exchange: “But will it be a paean or a dirge?” “Depends how well we do, I guess.” “Best not fail, then.”
III

West: Vault of the Beamcasters

A lone explorer in a self-repaired suit descends into an abandoned mine for gems, and finds another people, another light, and a deal nobody asked him about.

West, the world of Vault of the Beamcasters

Pocket mines and the Great Shutdown

Like the North, the West’s storyline comes in three parts: Promesst 1–3, internally promesst1_*, promesst2_*, promesst3_*. In each part the Caver finds his own artifact (the rod, the torc, the prism), and the dragon goes through its own stage: egg-stone, hungry hatchling, grown emissary. According to Thinky Games, the Promesst games themselves were “directly inspired by Corrypt from Michael Brough.”

The mines of this world are “pockets”: the space wraps around, and when the Caver reaches its edge he comes out on the other side. “Pocket size confirmed. It’s manageable” (promesst1_wrap). One sign in the Warehouse even gives the exact size of such a pocket: “23 x 24” (sign_ortho2_pocket_size).

The mines once ran on Beamcasters, machines that take gems and emit colored beams with special properties. Then came the Great Shutdown: “They shut down in such a panic, they just left all the gems” (promesst1_gems_amount1). “Why it collapsed is a secret,” says the Caver; his people “can’t build new Beamcasters, any more” (sp_gem_transport_end). The deeper he goes, the more architecture there is, although “the tomes said buildings were rare in pocket mines”: “This mine is special...” (promesst2_buildings). According to a note, it is temple construction: “More temple. Ever stranger” (promesst1_red_beam_lower). The word “magic” doesn’t exist here at all: Isidore asks about it, and to the Caver “‘magic’ meant ‘fairy tale’” (hp_wizard_torus_3).

The Caver and his suit

The hero has no name: in the credits he is “the Caver,” in the subtitles gemsman, in the code’s list of characters moonman code, and in the judges’ lines of the third ending “the Explorer.” Voiced by Sean Rohani. He has refurbished an old gemsuit. Its “shifter” miniaturizes gems so they fit on the rack and enlarges them again for use; as he explains to Tif, “we slide the lattice.” The suit can’t turn around in a narrow passage, and the hero has five calm lines on the subject, from “The gemsuit’s too bulky; can’t turn without space” to “Suit, you bless me, and you curse me” (promesst1_gemsuit_cannot_turn_*). A note explains why they are calm: the situation is frustrating enough for the player, so the hero himself shouldn’t be.

The most elegant explanation in this world is what the suit is made of. Isidore expects lacquered wood, metal at the joints, charged stones; but wood, metal and stone are exactly what blocks the beams. The suit is “made of animal bone, leather, sewn with sinews”: the beams were made to permeate people, and the workmen “needed to stand inside the beams while wearing the suits” (sp_gem_transport).

Sometimes you end up somewhere that would have seemed weird, beforehand.

The Caver on how the suit was designed, sp_gem_transport

It’s a line about design in general, and it sounds like the motto of the whole game inference

As the gems pile up, the Caver keeps explaining to himself that this isn’t greed: the gems are for research; well, he will sell some, but to hire a staff; well, he deserves some comfort too, “It’s hard to summon great ideas when the rent falls due and the wallet is empty”; and finally, “It seems there’s no stopping me!” (promesst1_gems_amount1–7). He destroys the old Rovers with a short “Justified. We’ll learn to build new ones.” By the end of the game he has built a second suit himself, the “Mark 2,” and entrusts it to his cousin (sp_friendly_nudges).

Three artifacts

  • “A rod of alabaster, fixed onto a leather cuff,” with metallic fittings (promesst1_wand_get). “This thing is so powerful. Why’d they bury it?” According to the fan guide, the rod moves the object in front of you to the next free tile, through walls and gates. The developer commentary calls it “the Displacer,” and a judge in the third ending calls it “that dangerous zapper,” which can send things off the screen inference In the hybrid zones the heroes pick it up too: the Druid is happy to “be a mover, for once,” while the Bard notes that “Brute force is not normally my way.”
  • “A lucite torc, to be worn about the neck” (promesst2_torc_get). “Useful! Wish I’d had this thing earlier.” Per the fan guide, it turns the beams that hit you toward the direction you face.
  • “A triangular prism on a chain, worn as a pendant” (promesst3_prism_get). It switches an emitter’s color. In the third ending the player is asked to use it to choose which beam colors “create the most interesting puzzle” (judge_best_beam_colors_0).

A vocabulary of beams

Save names in the game are drawn at random from each world’s vocabulary, and the western list reads like an index of its concepts: Through, Smash, Open, Double, Endpoint, Rover, Elevator, Gem, Alter, Dragon, Lizard, Egg, Strong, Squeeze (campaign_name_p_*). The tasks of the third ending show how they’re used: Smash breaks stones, Open opens gates, Strong and Smash can be “used together in an unexpected way” (judge_end3_redundant_beams_1), and Through, Endpoint and Double are the tools for one-way doors (judge_make_oneway_*). In a Double Beam, the Wizard can swap “with positions that he couldn’t normally” reach (sign_simplify_wizard_0). Rovers are little machines with simple automatic controls that can drive into the spot you just left.

Endpoint has a terrible edge case. If a beam runs to the edge of the level with nothing to stop it (on the Overworld, which wraps around, that can only happen straight up), its far end lies, in the code’s own words, “at infinity” (endpoint_b_is_at_infinity) code Whoever steps along such a beam is carried off the map. The level’s doors break, the sound goes muffled, the level can no longer be won, and only undo brings the hero back. This is not a glitch left in; it is what an unbent rule costs. Blow refuses to cheat his mechanics (“of course we’re not going to cheat to make something happen that’s a little bit against the rules of how the beams work”), and he describes this very case without a flinch: “The left end of the beam goes to infinity, so if you go left, you disappear and lose the level” (Wookash Podcast). The heroes turn it into a weapon. In the level endpoint_weapon, a director’s note describes what happens to a goblin: it disappears “into the infinite, essentially disintegrating it.” Tif: “Just ... gone ... I almost feel bad for the goblins.” The Bard: “They asked for it, didn’t they” (hp_endpoint_weapon_mid1). What saves the Visitor from the same fate on the way up to the sky is a single ring of mirrors (chapter VIII).

The egg, the hatchling and the deal

At the bottom of the first mine lies a large stone, “Mottled, polished ... iridescent?” (promesst1_egg). The Caver wonders whether it has “something to do with the Great Shutdown,” and suspects the stone was “placed to seal” the way further down (promesst1_further_down). A voice comes from inside it: “Return with gems. Gems bring life!” The Caver decides “the depths are getting to me.” When a beam falls on the stone, it begins to glow, and a dragon hatches (promesst1_end).

The hatchling wants to call them “foods,” but forces itself to say “gems” so it will be understood. The gems taste “cold. Cold and good”; “Energy builds inside,” “The cold light is growing.” Yet it tempers its hunger and insists on keeping nine until the end, because the puzzle will still need them (promesst2_keep_some_*). In the code the hatchling has four states, “just fed,” “eager wait,” “waiting further away” and “wandering,” plus a separate mark for having refused to eat code The grown dragon tells the essential part:

You open mines, we extrude nidi. Sometimes these turn out to have been the same.

The Dragon, promesst3_start
  • The dragons have a ritual saying: “Our light crosses your light.” “Your people used to know this!”
  • Humans learned to build artificial beam producers from the dragons. “At least my people have hygiene. We don’t throw our beams just everywhere” (promesst3_gems_amount_3).
  • Contact between the peoples was lost “several lifetimes of one such as yourself” ago. “Our people do not maintain a grudge about those times. Do yours?” (promesst3_gems_amount_4).
  • It hears its kin “far away and faint,” and they thank the Caver for hatching it and helping it grow. He is at a loss: “I ... it was ... I was just going forward?” (promesst3_second_room).
  • The dragon’s role is “to seek more places where our volumes can superpose. Strengthen and enlarge,” and to bring humans “new light.”
  • It was supposed to return home with the Caver as an emissary. “But then, a deal was struck. Lavish expenditure. You will not return directly home, not yet” (promesst3_gems_amount_7). Parting words: “Until next time.”

hypothesisDragons are natural builders of space

Extruding “nidi” that coincide with pocket mines and seeking places where “volumes can superpose” is exactly what the Order does, only here it is a trait of a species. That makes the dragons natural partners of the Order, and, judging by the “lavish expenditure” formula, it was with them that the Order made its deal about the Caver. The Great Shutdown was not first contact, though: humans learned to build beam producers from the dragons, and the dragon is indignant that they no longer remember it (“Your people used to know this!”). The Shutdown looks more like the rupture itself: a panic that left the gems lying in the mines, a cause kept “secret,” and a dragon asking, pointedly, whether humans still hold a grudge “about those times.” A people that learned its craft from its neighbors, broke with them and then forgot they existed is a darker story than a scare. And the iridescent egg at the bottom may literally have been a seal on the passage, just as the Caver suspected.

South: Skipping Stones to Lonely Homes

The game’s chattiest pair, a sailor and her ship, get stuck at a nameless archipelago and along the way expose the Guild’s best-kept secret.

South, the world of Skipping Stones to Lonely Homes

The sea, the Guild and spirit-ships

An endless ocean dotted with islands. The water here is dense: according to Leamington, it will drown a man at the mere depth of his own height. Special stones skip across it. When Isidore asks what makes them go, Janaila answers in an “are you five?” tone: “Motive principle.” “It’s what makes the stones go” (hs_island_friends_2). A stone flies until it hits something; lily pads drift with the current, and the rivers feed into a whirlpool, “the biggest mouth you’ve ever seen.”

Everything here is run by the Guild. Its ships are alive: each one houses a spirit from an immaterial world where, as Janaila puts it, “there’s nothing there, nothing that we would call a thing,” and yet “he says a lot of things happen” (hs_warrior_sailor_3). The Guild builds the spirit a body and binds it into special filaments threaded through the hull; in return it scries dangers ahead and drives the ship at tremendous speed. The runners are carved from skipping stones, and the steering is done by control struts made of a “special different stone,” “quite sensitive and pliant,” which obeys the spirit’s will (ssnew_built_in_2). Borraidus himself is “a betting djinn” and “a bonded carrier of the Guild.”

Janaila and Borraidus

Janaila (voiced by Erica Luttrell) is a “Footsailor,” the one who goes ashore and deals with local circumstances. Her joke about herself: “Sailor school is mostly knots.” From his first lines, Borraidus (voiced by Jamieson K Price) calls her “fleshy one” and sends her to “fetch” a map, which earns him: “Fetch? Shall I also scratch my fleas and bark at the parcelier?” She then keeps a tally of his offenses (“Four offenses”), and he calmly replies that as “a bonded carrier of the Guild” he “can always tally.” Midway through the storyline she starts calling him “Borrie,” and he threatens to sail off without her.

The interdict: the Guild’s secret

The storyline opens with the ship freezing at full speed: it has been “interdicted.” Later Borraidus explains how it works, and it’s one of the best passages in the script. An interdict is just writing, symbols on a wall or in the sand. The stones emit nothing. Every Guild ship’s control struts “have embedded scriers that constantly watch for interdiction marks; one glimpse and the control struts lock rigid”: the ship stops itself (ssnew_built_in_1–3). For the ship it is almost physically painful, while the sailor herself is untouched: “I’ve never been interdicted before.” “You weren’t!” (ssnew_interdiction_first).

Self-sabotage.

Janaila, ssnew_built_in_2

Hence the corollary: against an enemy fleet an interdict is useless, since “no enemy would self-sabotage.” It is a safeguard against theft and mutiny: the vaults are opened only for the “theft of a critical Boat” or when “an insurrection needs quelling.” The public believes “false tales of interdictions stopping an invasion fleet cold in the water,” and the Guild keeps the writing locked in dark vaults, because anyone who can copy it can lay interdicts of their own. According to a note, Janaila’s eyes narrow at this point: is the Guild spreading the falsehood on purpose? Why does Borraidus give it all away? “If we die here, then it didn’t matter, eh.”

Part one: the archipelago

  1. The ship freezes by islands that are “just a smudge on the charts.” The nearest outpost is a week away. Janaila goes for the map; Borraidus scries through the fog to a single spot to the south, where the interdict lies.
  2. Borraidus suspects traps and hints evasively that the islands have “features”: “Gold, and Conflux” (ssnew_no_traps). “Dockside palm-readers give more for a copper,” Janaila grumbles. He later explains both hints in turn, “when the time comes”: “Gold” is the golden platform, which he introduces as “one of those things I haven’t yet told you about,” and “Conflux,” a word for the flowing together of rivers, is apparently the whirlpool the river runs into, which he brings up with “It’s time for me to bother you again” inference
  3. One island holds a big golden platform. In Borraidus’s scry, “tendrils of energy” that should connect somewhere here instead “go off... as if toward another world”: “Someone wants us to go there.” Janaila touches the center, lights bloom, and “a great pulse went out, toward some other place.” “A message, perhaps. A short one. What it said? No idea” (skipping_stones_shrine). And it does go to the Warehouse. In the code the platform is an Overworld_Unlock, and its whole message is one bit, “this level is partly done,” which thaws cages on the Overworld map (chapter I) code
  4. The river doesn’t skirt the target isle, so she has to keep “tacking” and come from the other side, and the river itself feeds a gigantic whirlpool. Borraidus asks: “If you are swallowed, there will be no way home for me. Think of me, please.” A note explains that a stranded ship would eventually unbind and its spirit go back to the spirit world, unless someone happened by. “Thanks, boat. I’ll figure something out.” “You often do” (skipping_stones_whirlpool).
  5. If Janaila returns to the ship without breaking the interdict, Borraidus is baffled: “You went right by the island with the interdict, but you didn’t disarm it? What kind of a joke is this?” She has no explanation: “I’m not sure what happened... I don’t ... hmm” (chapter VI). Then he winks at the player: “If only there were some way to change the past.” “Dear, I’m from the spirit world. I know some things” (skipping_stones_return_no_impeller). A note confirms he is talking about the game’s undo, slightly breaking the fourth wall.
  6. The interdict is broken, but “there’s a residual effect”: the ship can sail away from the isles but not farther in. A storm “of incredible power” is coming.

Six treasures

As the storm approaches, Janaila finds a chest that the scry can’t see. Borraidus has heard of such things: “a special insulating chest that occults from scry.” With one example to work from, he can sense the rest: “when the boxes hide the space inside, it makes little bumps in the aether.” There are six chests in all (skipping_stones_treasures_1), and each one holds a charting implement with an impossible property.

Structurally, it is the classic optional reward. Borraidus warns right away: “To get them all, you’d have to be quite shrewd.” “I’m always quite shrewd!” (skipping_stones_treasures_1). And the chests are buried suspiciously shallow. The one Janaila found “was peeking out of the sand a bit,” and “No decent sailor would bury a strongbox less than three feet under. Double that, if he’s actually serious.” Borraidus allows that storms may “have been gradually unearthing them” (skipping_stones_done_treasures_1).

On the Overworld the finds are put on display code: six plinths stand near the Crossroads and fill up in order as you find more, so the game cares how many chests you found, not which. Beyond that, treasures give nothing: no achievement, no rank, no completion percentage, no open gates. It is a reward for curiosity, not for progress.

hypothesisA cartographer’s kit for impossible spaces

All six objects are “implements for charting,” “pieces of the same set.” A ruler that changes its scale would suit spaces that stretch; pins engraved with scenes look like markers for a hundred different places; and chests that hide space are little pockets themselves. The Acting Director complains that from afar humanity’s living space looks like “spatial steel wool,” and “it’s a serious challenge to make maps, even of the well-known parts” (audiolog_sky_wool). The sundial with a shadow from a foreign light is especially curious: the Order’s time is “clocked by our Central Obelisk” (sign_osst), and an obelisk is the gnomon of a giant sundial. Perhaps the dial shows the Order’s time rather than local time.

hypothesis…or a level editor’s toolbar

The same set also reads as a designer’s tools. A blank parchment nothing sticks to: an empty canvas. A ruler that stretches, spreading its markings evenly: the grid scale. A hundred pins engraved with scenes that line themselves up in parallel: a palette of ready-made scenes with snap-to-grid. A sundial whose shadow ignores the actual light: lighting set from outside. Ink that cannot spill and a pen whose tips run from a fine point to a brush and a blotter: brushes and an eraser. And the chests are buried as shallowly as if they had been placed by whoever laid the archipelago out for its trials, following the same rules by which the Acting Director places her recordings (chapter IX).

But who hid the chests, the Order, the mysterious Farguild or someone else, the game doesn’t say.

Part two: the Surveyor and Vedessud

Janaila answers someone else’s call for help: the ship Vedessud (“Veddy”) is stuck between two interdicts at once. Its owner, the Surveyor (voiced by Anjali Bhimani), with unusually white hair, introduces herself as a native of the Eastern Reaches, “about as far as the Guild has ever mapped,” “six months’ journey if the weather be kind,” near the Deadwaters. The Deadwaters are a sea that “shines like polished onyx, dead flat,” and “stops stones dead in their tracks. They cannot skip. All that energy, just eaten.” Young men went out there on dares; “Usually they all came back. But it is the times they do not that make the reputation” (pals_deadwaters).

She says she got into the Guild through a romance with a diplomat from the Mainland; a director’s note explains that this is part of her cover, a story of a provincial woman self-conscious about her roots (pals_where_from_2). Borraidus keeps an eye on her from the start: he has Janaila ask her about the interdicts and, according to a note, watches to “see if she responds suspiciously in any way.” Her theory: “Perhaps they’re just old tests, made during initial development of the technique, now forgotten,” “and newly uncovered.” Janaila finds it “implausible,” and at that very moment the Surveyor, for some reason, shushes her own ship (a note elsewhere makes clear that only a ship’s own sailor can hear it): “Shhh, Veddy, I’m trying to talk” (pals_asking_about_interdicts_1–2).

Then she confides her “secondary duties”: to look for evidence of an incursion by the Farguild, “an empire beyond the Deadwaters,” a presumed entity with no known name. “If these interdicts were stolen, we may now have that evidence” (pals_farguild). When they finally meet, Veddy warmly praises Janaila, and the Surveyor is embarrassed. If the sailors end up on the wrong ships, they agree to “meet out at sea and do a hot swap.” If they don’t, Borraidus remarks as they part that she “seems every bit as inventive and resourceful as you. But under the surface, there’s something odd...” “Besides the hair, you mean?” “Besides the hair.” “We’ll talk at sea” (pals_sailors_not_swapped).

Inside the Warehouse, Borraidus warns that she is here too. Janaila had thought she was the only one who could get into this inter-world space, but “The iridescence admits her, like it did you. That’s all we know about how and why” (double_agent_intro). And the Guild knows something about her that is “... significant”: a note calls that an understatement, and Borraidus is “shocked they held it back this long” (double_agent_intro_end). Janaila tries to draw her out: “So ... Borraidus told me who you really are.” “Did he? I doubt he knows the whole story.” “Want to help mend my ignorance?” “Not at this time.” A note explains that she is not permitted to say more. Borraidus has told Janaila to trust her anyway, “for now,” and the Surveyor agrees: “We have shared interests here,” meaning, per the note, in this whole space the Order has built (sp_double_agent). At the end of the game Borraidus asks Vedessud whether she ever imagined they’d see a place such as this. “I did, actually.” “You ... did?” Per the note, he is stunned, wondering what incredible depth of machinations would be necessary for this to be true (end3_boats).

hypothesisThe Surveyor comes from beyond the Deadwaters

She speaks noticeably more often than others in full, bookish forms (“it is,” “I am,” “you cannot”) and admits that “where I come from,” local turns of phrase are uncommon. Her hair is white, and according to a director’s note her boat is faster than Janaila’s. Her job is close to a perfect cover: surveying means she doesn’t “have to deal with anyone too important,” she is “not good at making friends,” and she never hauls valuables anyone would attack her for (pals_job_2, pals_job_3). Her provincial biography is a cover, a note says she “infiltrated the Eastern Reaches under unknown circumstances,” and Vedessud had imagined the Warehouse in advance. She is “not permitted” to tell more about herself, so someone stands behind her. All this is best explained if she came from the Farguild itself and is searching for an “incursion” in her own interests. None of it is confirmed outright.

hypothesis…or the Bard, years later

A bolder reading goes further: the Surveyor is the Bard, older and in the Order’s service. The clues are odd and specific. One actor, Anjali Bhimani, voices both, and the Surveyor’s hair is “an obvious and unusual white color” (pals_where_from_1). On the cliffs she meets the heights with “Oh my God!” and later sums up: “If you don’t look, there is nothing there. Simple.” Those are Valens’s exclamation and the Druid’s catchphrase, the two companions the Bard traveled with, and the director’s notes flag both: “This is Warrior’s line; she should not say it,” “She also probably shouldn’t say ‘Simple.’ Druid said that” (3d_intro_staircase, 3d_intro_carry). The Bard is the hero who borrows other people’s words to meet them on their ground, and a woman who has spent her life becoming what others want would make a natural undercover recruiter. It would also give the South what the other three worlds have, a broker, here posing as a woman in need of rescue: her ship was stuck between two interdicts when she called for help, and “Thank you for answering my call!” are her first words (pals_start). Her “secondary duties,” finding evidence of an incursion from beyond the Deadwaters, would then be a hunt for her own side. The objections are real. The notes treat both echoes as slips to fix, not clues to plant; the Surveyor says she is “not good at making friends”; and nothing in the files ties the two women together except the voice.

Other details of the storyline

  • Borraidus looks at the Rovers from the world of beams and wonders whether their “decision-making apparatus” could “be inhabited by a mind such as mine” (sp_rover_mission). Then: “I’ve hatched a plan that we must discuss.” “Not sure I like the sound of that!” (sp_rover_mission_end). Whether he ever tries it, the files don’t say.
  • In a space that repeats itself, the link to him goes silent: “Borraidus? Are you there?” Janaila has to manage on her own (sp_wrapping_level_intro).
  • In the final part he returns: “I’m here to see you through this final phase.” “But ... how do you know what’s happening?” (end3_skipping_1).

hypothesisThe interdicts as a designer’s work

The interdict stops the ship and forces the sailor to cross every puzzle on foot, and the golden platform on the same islands betrays the Warehouse’s presence. Borraidus says it outright: “Someone wants us to go there.” Internally, the interdict is even called an impeller, something that drives you forward (skipping_stones_approaching_impeller, skipping_stones_return_no_impeller). An interdict is a sign that controls the behavior of whoever sees it, just like the signs Leamington uses to guide the Visitor in the second ending. And the third ending teaches the same lesson: puzzle design “is also about making sure that the player performs these interactions,” and “one of the simplest ways to enforce that the player does what you want is to make doors that only open in one direction” (sign_make_oneway_1–2).

East: The Mirror Isles

The quietest and most personal storyline: a rich man, convinced he will soon die, returns to the place where, as a boy, he fled his trial.

East, the world of The Mirror Isles

The isles of the Mirrorwrights

The archipelago belongs to the College of the Mirrorwrights, the makers of magic mirrors. Teenagers take their trial here; the public knows only the first trial, but the College has other “sequences” as well. The traditional greeting is “Keeping busy?”, answered with the same words. Above it all stand the Oracles, who are known to be “precise, and tricky” (mirror_oracle_4b).

The Trader

The Trader (voiced by Andrew Kishino) failed the trial as a boy: when the mirrors began to split him, he panicked and fled. According to a director’s note, inside the mirrors the candidate is attacked by “some kind of psychological entity” that assaults the ego and tries to break you down (mirror_pre_clones). He spent his whole life rebuilding himself: hoarded pennies from odd jobs, opened a market stall, invested its profit in a first modest shipping route, and grew it into a whole fleet. He says himself that through decades of success he tried to “reassemble, glue, tape over” his own shattered glass, but “had never truly made whole” (mirror_stranger_before_hm_1).

The prophecy and the stranger

Years ago, an Oracle predicted that he would become “the most successful Tradesman,” and it came to pass. Now she told him:

You will depart this world before the year is out. You must order your affairs.

mirror_oracle_3

Convinced he would die, he walked for the first time in years into a tavern he passed every day. There a stranger asked for his story: “fair of complexion,” with mannerisms “as though he’d set foot on many shores, and come away carrying a bit of each,” dressed as a commoner, “but everything he wore was oddly crisp” (mirror_stranger_3).

The stranger said that he possessed “judgement refined at a level far exceeding the typical man,” and could therefore plainly see that a broken shard of the Trader lay “glinting in the sand of the Mirror Isles,” and that he would never be whole until he went back to reclaim it (mirror_stranger_6). He must go back, “even if it were very difficult to eventuate and took lavish expenditure,” if he were “to depart this world a full and self-respecting man” (mirror_stranger_double_mirrors). The Trader had “two drinks too many,” and by the time the sun had peaked the plan was in motion: he diverted one of his own ships along a circuitous route, because booking passage to the Isles with no official business is “unheard of.”

A Mirrorwright from the same cohort

On the Isles he is met by the Mirrorwright (voiced by David Cui Cui): “These are strange times. We’ve kept quiet. Well. I’m here to state that we are unoffended by your presence” (mirror_watcher_first). Later it turns out that they were in the same cohort: he ran the trial immediately after the Trader, read “the contortions on your face” as the boy was being led away, understood what danger to expect, and steeled himself against it. “So, you helped me succeed” (mirror_pre_clones). His “Same is same,” a note explains, means not only “the same cohort” but “we two are much the same as each other.” When the Trader recounts the prophecy, the Mirrorwright asks whether he is sure that “depart this world” meant die; a note says he “knows something about this that he is not saying” (mirror_oracle_2). What he knew comes out a few scenes later: the Chiefs had already struck their deal, and the note to his announcement spells it out, “depart this world” is “an explanation for the prediction. He’s not going to die; he’s going to leave” (mirror_deal_2_end). The man who met the Trader on the shore was the Chiefs’ messenger from his first word inference

The mechanics stage this exactly. In the Mirror Isles, reflections that land on the same tile merge back into one, so the Trader becomes whole the only way his clones can: by being brought to the same place at the same moment. The judge of the third ending demands the same discipline of a designer: “Eliminate any possibility of clones diverging” (judge_end2_clone_divergence_1). The deal: “depart this world,” literally

The Mirrorwright brings good news: “You’re not going to die. At least, not anytime too soon.” A deal has been struck between the Chief Mirrorwrights “and ... someone else.” There is “a special mirror set within a perimeter of polychromic light,” and only the Trader can use it, “as only you have been invited.” Invited by whom? “That is not given me to know.” Why him? “You were chosen by the counterparty” (mirror_deal_2). And the prophecy comes true in the literal sense:

That’s the good news! You’ve been invited to ... depart this world.

The Mirrorwright, mirror_deal_2_end

The Chiefs show him a sequence that initiates usually go through “around age 16”: “They said you’ll need these ideas where you’re going” (mm_why). And it turns out they have already promoted him to Apprentice Mirrorwright. “I just push these things around. I know nothing about making them.” “You will eventually.” “It’s been foreseen. So they tell me” (mm_how). Even in the Warehouse the Chiefs send word through the Mirrorwright: “Just keep going. When the time comes, we’ll give you word.” “I don’t like being kept in the dark” (3d_intro_mirrors). At the end of the game he is almost home: “I wonder how she’ll react when she finds out I’m not dead” (end3_mirror_isles_second). The game never says who “she” is, but the Oracle is the weaker candidate inference She chose her words, and if she had meant “die,” “she could have just said that. Oracles are precise, and tricky” (mirror_oracle_4b): she of all people would not be surprised. That leaves the wife his clone dismissed with “Wife doesn’t care for me.” It is a small, sad line to end on: the man who came back whole wonders how the woman who doesn’t care for him will take the news that he lives.

The stranger in the tavern is Reynard

No name is spoken, but the matches pile up inference The stranger boasts of his refined “judgement,” Reynard’s central theme. He uses the signature phrase “very difficult to eventuate and took lavish expenditure,” and a translator’s note insists that it be rendered the same way everywhere “so that the player can match them up” (mirror_stranger_double_mirrors). His mannerisms, “as though he’d set foot on many shores, and come away carrying a bit of each,” are an almost literal portrait of an Order agent who travels between worlds. In the coda, Janaila describes Reynard as “the theatrical guy. Judgy.” The Trader, who has just asked “Who?”, is scripted to come back with “That fellow?”, apparently recognizing the man from the description (all_together_now_reassembly_1). Reynard dressed up as a commoner to bring one more piece to the Warehouse.

The subtitle file doesn’t have the Trader’s question: in its place is a note, “Todo: Missing line,” so Tif’s “That fellow” answers a question nobody asked (more traces like this in chapter XI).

hypothesisOne operation, two voices

The prophecy and the stranger may be parts of one maneuver. The Oracle’s words sent a man who had passed a tavern daily for years through its door, and inside sat the one stranger ready to turn a death sentence into a voyage; the deal then made the prophecy literally true. The Oracle had earned his trust “years back” with a forecast that came to pass, and when the Trader mentions her, the Mirrorwright “knows something about this that he is not saying” (mirror_oracle_2). Was she the Order’s partner, its unwitting tool or, in the boldest version, the Acting Director herself? The game never shows her.

  • He tells Janaila that “the mirrors: they’ll make you feel alone”; later she admits, “I haven’t been this alone in a long time. Peaceful, but sad” (ms_alone_3). A note adds that their exchange has “maybe a hint of romantic potential (but not a lot, as Trader is a loner and also married).”
  • He reasons that the Warehouse’s mirrors weren’t made by the Mirrorwrights: “the Mirrorwrights did not build the place we are now. So the mirrors must not be theirs either” (mirror_transport_4).

When worlds collide

In the hybrid zones, heroes from different worlds see each other for the first time. The sharpest observations about how the worlds work are made here, when an outsider asks about what a local takes for granted.

First meetings

The worlds_collide_* scenes all follow one pattern: two characters are stuck, each in their own mechanics, and they can only get out together. Each such level stands beside the tower of its pair, and it is this level that earns the “gold” achievement (on Steam, “Gold HxM” and the like, though the description there promises opening a gold room) code Showing the Heroes-and-Mirrors meeting on stream, Blow put the idea plainly: “I’ve played enough mirrors that I know how that goes, and I’ve played enough Heroes that I know how that goes,” and the level makes “this spotlight out of putting them both together for the first time” (livestream with Edmund McMillen).

Conversations that explain the worlds

  • Isidore and the doughnut-shaped space. The Caver mentioned wrapping spaces in passing; for Isidore it’s a revolution. In his world, the strength of magic runs into a conflict: energy must “cycle, through a medium such as a crystal, in order to compound,” but turning to loop through the crystal “reduces the speed of energy; the slowdown saps potency.” “In a place that repeats without curving, this conflict dissolves. Energy can fly fast and true.” “Perhaps there is no limit! I must learn to create one of these spaces” (hp_wizard_torus_4–6). And right away: this “could mean more for all of us than any of that political nonsense,” while the King only “pretends to care.”
  • Isidore and the stone that never stops. He asks Janaila: if a stone never hit anything, say in a place that connects end to end “somewhat like a ... a doughnut,” would it ever stop? “Then it would fly off the doughnut.” “No, the doughnut is perfectly straight and goes forever!” “You’re a strange man” (hs_island_friends_3_end). The answer to his question is on the map: the lower tier has a zone called Endless Skipping.
  • Tif and the Caver on crystals. The gems of the West stop beams thanks to a “specially-shaped internal lattice,” which the suit can “slide”; the crystals of the North apparently have a different shape inside. Tif doesn’t care: “The gems shine, the crystals shine. Different from each other, each beautiful in its own way” (thief_promesst_crystals).
  • Valens and Janaila on the ship. “So, your best friend is a ‘boat’.” “Really, ‘boat’ is a casual term. He’s a ship capable of crossing great oceans!” Valens explains that in his world vehicles are steered by people, and can’t see why you’d leave the helm to a spirit; Janaila explains she can manage open ocean herself, but “threading isles and reefs at speed... I’m only human, good Sir” (hs_warrior_sailor_*).
  • The Druid and Janaila. They compare the spirit in a ship with the spirit in a transmuted body. Could she learn what he does? “From me? No. From someone else? Officially, no. Unofficially... it’s complicated” (hs_sailor_druid_3_end). One more confirmation that the druids’ “inborn ability” is a cover story.
  • Janaila and the Surveyor on the cliffs. Both come from a flat sea world and are seeing heights for the first time: “Is this what the cityfolk feel like when they say they’re ‘seasick’?” Later they haul rocks on their shoulders and tease each other, and in the end admit they miss the same thing: “I’m so pent up. No ship to berate.” “You know, I just realized. Me too” (3d_intro_carry_actual_b).
  • Tif and the Bard in the burned town. The Bard recognizes the western town where her three happy years in the West began, and the market square she visited “every few days in that first year” (hp_bard_market_square). The town is burning and full of goblins. Tif wonders (hp_endpoint_weapon_mid2): did they “ravage the town, then transport it here,” or is this “a duplicate, timber for timber, built to be burned,” while “the original remains unscathed”? The Bard says it feels like “happy memories, burned like an old manuscript that was fake; or a manuscript that was real, but I treated it as a fake” (hp_endpoint_weapon_mid5). For a heroine who has spent her life playing the role others need, it is a precise image. This may be the very town the goblins overran in Heroes 2 while the Caravan dawdled (heroes2_warrior_dragon_intro): to a woman from beyond the canyon, Valens’s kingdom is “the West.” Against it: neither Tif nor Isidore recognizes the place, and Isidore knows it only from the Bard’s stories and songs (hp_wizard_be_aggro) hypothesis
  • Who fears what. Janaila and the Caver compare the worst dangers of their worlds: “stuck in an orange beam,” “stranded on an island,” “sucked into a giant whirlpool,” “getting rematerialized into a wall.” Both find the dragon-filled world of the heroes “so ... deadly” (obf_monster_drop).

The heroes sense the player

  • Leamington says it plainly: “I feel as though I’m making the decisions ... but I’m sure they believe the decisions are theirs. We can’t both be right” (audiolog_g_possession).
  • Isidore suspects “some mind behind the mind,” “a background mind that creates misadventures just to steer us where it wants us” (heroes2_wizard_and_priestess_alone_second).
  • Tif: “Sometimes I feel like I’m being watched. It’s creepy, but, maybe a little nice? Someone cares about me!” (heroes1_5). And later, in a zone built to make her go to great lengths: “Sometimes I just feel like I’m being taunted!” (end3_thief_taunted).
  • Valens: “Lately I’ve been getting so confused. I don’t know where I am, I don’t know which direction to go. Sometimes it takes a lot of faffing about before I find a good direction. But I suppose I haven’t failed yet” (rotator). That is almost word for word the player’s experience with a hard puzzle.
  • Isidore, in a second, nearly identical level: “Does this seem familiar to you?” The Caver, per the note, has “No idea what Wizard is talking about,” and Isidore, reprising his speech about the suit, trails off mid-sentence (sp_gem_transport_2, sp_gem_transport_2_end).   • Borraidus and the undo button: “If only there were some way to change the past.”
  • Janaila, when the player walks her right past the interdict and back to the ship: “I’m not sure what happened... I don’t ... hmm” (skipping_stones_return_no_impeller). Here the move was so plainly someone else’s that the explanation breaks down. VII

The Overworld and the Order

Above the four worlds there is a fifth: the one in which they were assembled. Its story is told through one explorer’s beacons, the conversations of the Order’s two leaders, and what the Queen sees.

Leamington: the explorer who became a keeper

Leamington (voiced by George Ackles) is a scientist from a world where doorsteads, portals that “connect space itself,” have been “common technology” for a hundred years; the special doorstead his group built “uses hundreds” of gears, fitted and “precisely tuned.” His country travels by the “binary partitioning” of National Transport, which turns a journey into “a sequence of skips, which create a linear path, like a road” (audiolog_g_musing_on_fast_travel). It sounds like a programmer’s joke: a route that keeps getting cut in half, then in half again, is binary search applied to transit hypothesis There are no computers there: Leamington knows the Mandelbrot set only from “still images in books and papers,” as “the Clayson’s Field.” His oath is “By the Book!”, and “with cold hands” in his language means “in cold blood.” His team aimed their doorstead at a “pronounced peak in the sounding-outs” between worlds, and the Warehouse, he realizes, was “advertising” itself with that peak (intro_before_teleporter).

The doorstead closed right behind him. He leaves recorded beacons for his colleague, Mr. Quigley, and travels from scientific delight to loneliness:

  1. First observations. Plaques “in an unknown language” whose meaning he somehow just knows. Gold that can’t be precious to its builders. Worlds “chosen according to the kinds of events that happen inside them.”
  2. Big questions. “Where does this place exist, wherein I now stand?” The laws of Nature of the Warehouse can’t conflict with those of worlds A and B, so its substrate is either “a subset of A and B, that can yet be built outward,” or “a superset of A and B, that is then trimmed back” (audiolog_g_substrate). And why is no one here?
  3. Strange things about the body. He feels no hunger or thirst: “You know I love a good brisket, but here the physical hunger just never arises.” Parsons, who “always complains of the nuisance of eating,” would love it here.
  4. The watcher. He feels “judged by a watchful eye whose nature I know not,” and catches himself “courting that Watcher’s approval” in the topics he chooses and the precise words he employs (audiolog_g_being_watched). A note says he is no longer addressing only Quigley, but whoever is listening, all but hinting: if I know you’re there, maybe the game is up, and we can move on to the next phase?
  5. Distance. Home is becoming “more like a half-remembered dream than a real place.” Unceasingly alone, he feels closer every day to these worlds and their inhabitants, though he cannot speak to them, and between the worlds he begins to see, faintly, “certain kinds of connections. Silken strands.” “Perhaps I don’t want to return, except to see for a moment how you’re all getting on.” A note detects some denial in this (audiolog_g_far_from_home).
  6. Crisis. Years have passed. Perhaps Quigley “shut the door with cold hands” to keep the fame and fortune? He bids him goodbye for good, glimpses “a shadowy figure in the distance, cloaked,” and prepares to “advance a few more steps” (audiolog_g_crisis). A note says this isn’t literal: he is close to the first ending and knows it is a turning point in how he may be treated.
  7. The meeting. The Acting Director appears in person and teases him at once: “... your manners, sir! That look could be taken in quite the wrong way” (audiolog_sky_take_a_tour). A note: she had been watching him, unseen, for a while.
  8. The rank of Sokoban. After long conversations she entrusts this Warehouse to him. Sixteen months of his own time after their last meeting, he is still patching the Warehouse against the decay and replays it “after every third” other Warehouse. In the second and third endings he is the one testing the Queen.

One of his predictions came true. He asked whether his arrival “was encouraged, as a means of their finding us,” and whether one day “one of these stars” would find him back home, “faced with some carefully constructed enigma about maneuvering a doorstead through a doorstead” (audiolog_g_world_selection_3). Patrick’s Parabox makes exactly that kind of puzzle, boxes inside boxes, and its author Patrick Traynor is among the game’s designers inference Later Reynard announces that he has connected to Leamington’s home world and is studying its “dynamics” as a possible lower-density substitution within the Tetratetrad (audiolog_s_magistrate).

One more detail explains why we hear his beacons at all. A visitor’s recordings would normally “be considered foreign objects and would be automatically removed upon your exit,” but the Acting Director makes an exception: “But today, I’ll allow it. They’re nice. They help the place feel lived-in” (audiolog_s_tradition_of_our_people_part_2b). That is why the Queen, walking the same paths later, finds Leamington’s beacons inference

The Queen: a heroine with almost no lines

We play the Overworld as the Queen (voiced by Emily O’Brien). The game never speaks her name; the promotional description does: “As Queen Effia, you are transported to a realm of curious magic, dangerous contraptions, and vicious monsters.” Her story opens and closes with the same scene. She prays before the relics, finally found, of the one whose cause she inherited: “I am your ardent disciple, heir to your charge.” If she survives this day, she will set the relics forth “for all to see, a beacon” and lead her people “into renewal”; if not, if “the road leads only into the Maw,” let her go alone, and let her men “return to their children and their wives.” The guardsman Jackays interrupts: “My Queen! Urgently!” A traitor’s arrow flies, there is a pop, and the Queen vanishes (pre_coda_scene_assembled).

She calls for her men (“Jackays! Conroe! Are you here? Any loyal soldier, stand count!”), reads a “foreign script” that she understands “somehow,” demands that the hosts show themselves, and addresses them by name: “You who call yourselves Sinking Star! If you want to help me, send me back to my men.” “The raid must be yet ongoing.” Of the very first sign she remarks: “Not too Heavenly, this” (intro_queen_sign). She asks a “foreign transport” to take her home and, arriving somewhere else, sighs: “I suppose that was too much to ask.” At the goblin fortifications she remarks: “I had thought thus about my own walls.” Each world strikes her with its own mood: the Heroes’ “Subtle miasma...,” the Mirror Isles’ “air of contemplation,” and at a gold room: “Seems like a place they want me to notice” (queen_og_heroes2, queen_og_mirror_isles, queen_og_gold_room_first). Her own first pass through a beam unsettles her: “Not sure I’ll ever get used to this, not when it’s happening to me,” rather than to the heroes she inhabits (queen_og_first_green_beam).

At a wardrobe she asks, “A choice of outfits?” In new clothes she feels “like ... a new person” (queen_og_wardrobe, queen_og_wardrobe_2). She is more right than she knows code On her own the Queen can do only what classic sokoban begins with: push one block at a time. Stepping out through a wardrobe’s door gives her one hero’s skill, pushing whole rows, pulling, swapping places, protecting, transmuting or charming, or else the rod or the torc; stepping back in through the door makes her herself again, and entering another wardrobe from behind lets her stack two skills. Under every outfit she stays herself: in the code QUEEN is a flag of identity that no wardrobe removes and that does nothing at all, except tell the game who she is.

Her near-silence is a design decision. Blow wanted the heroine to mirror the player: “As a player, you don’t know where you are… So we want the character to be someone who doesn’t know where they are.” In the demo she commented more as she walked; he cut some of it, because “at some point we just let you be free to have your own thoughts” (livestream with Edmund McMillen).

The Order did not invite her. In the first ending the Acting Director remarks: “we invited neither of you,” meaning neither Leamington nor her, and suggests it may be “decay-related” (end1_dimension_1). Speaking to the Queen herself is forbidden. What happened next is told in chapter VIII.

The Acting Director

Voiced by Helen Sadler. She is very old (a note to psalm_139) and “artificially strengthened” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2): a note explains that this means raised intelligence, ability to focus and improved health, and that Leamington “will before long be” strengthened too. She was born after the loss of Earth, into one of the “Lifeboat societies,” the Society for the Preservation of Civil Life, which, according to a note, sought to recreate English society from a little before the Victorian era up until 1913, and which eventually dissolved (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_3). She joined the Order in the era of the “2-combiners.” She rose from Sokoban to Implementor by creating her first Warehouse and, imitating the Founders, recorded a solemn “invocation” at its entrance. Gossips said she “sounded pretentious and hollow”; she kept it up out of pride, then fell out of the practice, and now, after her talks with Leamington, she re-records it “for the first time in many, many years”: a prayer from Paradise Lost (audiolog_sky_invocation).

She built this Warehouse over about ten years, and it was “the very limit of what I could do”: “Had I known it could be my last, I might have kept pushing” (audiolog_sky_why_so_big). She is “Acting” because the real Director is away. She likes the children’s rhyme “See you later, Alligator” and says “Let not your heart be troubled” (Christ’s words in John 14:1). She is, by all appearances, the woman who appeared to Reynard in multicolored light (chapter II). And she is the one who broke the rule against speaking with Visitors for Leamington’s sake, as she later admits: she enjoyed their conversations, but “they caused a kerfuffle.”

The Director-on-Sabbatical

Voiced by Ray Chase. The Director of the Static Brigade, in Leamington’s words “the still-titular Director.” He has spent about six years of his own time investigating the decay that is eating the Warehouses (audiolog_u_meeting). He has left the running of things to the Acting Director and offered that she “appoint a successor” if she is tired. He wants to talk with Leamington “for reasons relating to my inquiry,” apparently because Leamington arrived uninvited inference His “invocation” is verse about Leviathan from chapter 41 of the Book of Job (audiolog_u_job_41_fragments). His conversations cover old games, the “cement” between worlds, the Tetratetrad, the decay, chaos as a cipher and computable beauty; chapter IX goes through them. He signs off slyly: there was “a kerfuffle among the Preservation Crew” when Leamington “just showed up,” “But ... ... what they don’t know won’t hurt them” (audiolog_u_decay_4).

Reynard after the Heroes

The Magistrate’s last appearance is in a conversation between the Acting Director and Leamington (audiolog_s_magistrate). First Leamington admits that “the prospect of greatly prolonging my own life is ... quite a temptation,” and the Acting Director observes that “if you’re here for inauthentic reasons, it’ll become obvious.” Then Reynard walks up: “Found you at last!” By his proper time, “it’s been a good fourteen years” since the victory lap. “As you requested, I’ve been working at the four-four,” which is what the Order calls the Tetratetrad (the Director says “after the four-four is done,” audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1), “attempting to mend connections that have broken.” “Success is limited, but nonzero.”

He recognizes Leamington as “the Doorstead Fellow” and is indignant: “Outrageous how you just showed up, without an invitation. I at least had to be judged worthy of an invite.” Then to business: he has been “looking into his dynamics as a possible lower-density substitution within the four-four,” and the Acting Director promises to discuss it “in confidence.” To Leamington himself, Reynard announces that he has “connected back to your home”: “You can leave any time you want! From your diaries, it seems you’re quite eager.” The answer is short: “I’ll discuss.” According to a note, Leamington will talk it over with the Acting Director later and doesn’t want to give Reynard very much.

The words “lower-density” are the key. The worlds inside each tetrad, the Director says, “were chosen for maximum density — with an unusual number of affordances, higher even than any of the worlds in this Warehouse” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1), and the decay sets in because “the cross-correlation has become too dense” (audiolog_u_decay_3). So Reynard is proposing a remedy: swap one of the Tetratetrad’s densest worlds for a sparser one, a world of doorsteads. If the swap goes ahead, Leamington’s prediction will come true inside the game as well (above) inference

Talking with the Acting Director, the Director recalls her previous outlander promoted to Sokoban, who “seems to have acclimated pretty well, strange as he is”; a note specifies that this is Reynard (audiolog_u_meeting). So the Magistrate became a Sokoban before Leamington did. In the final scene of the game he organizes the Queen’s rescue and decides he has found his “new hobby.”

A history of the Order

The chronology comes from signs in the Lobby and on the upper tier. Dates are given in the OSST calendar, which “coincides retrospectively with AD until 2276.”

  1. c. 2005–2030The “Damascene School” is active. Its logo and decor will inspire the Order’s Founder (sign_original_star). On who they might be, see chapter XI.
  2. 2230The Order is founded. Its tools are “merely computational: simulated worlds on femtomachine-based parallel logic evaluators” (sign_timeline_1). In plain words, the Order began as a studio of very sophisticated computer games inference
  3. 2271“Technology for seeding and expansion” makes it possible to build “small scenarios inside newly seeded bulks,” where a bulk is the physicists’ term for the higher-dimensional space in which observable spacetime is embedded (sign_timeline_2). These are the “early ancestors” of the Warehouses.
  4. 2276“Temporal orthogonality” forces a separate calendar. An OSST year keeps the length of the 2275 terrestrial year, “as clocked by our Central Obelisk.” The count wasn’t reset to zero “because when others did that, it felt very pompous” (sign_osst).
  5. 2284The first Lobby appears, Theme 1a, “an homage to the works of the Damascene School.” The theme “later fell into disuse, but here we bring it back” (sign_lobby_1, sign_lobby_2).
  6. 2372“The modern age”: the Order first sources “an extant World” instead of creating its Warehouses from whole cloth (sign_timeline_3).
  7. 2378The Order revises its emblem: between the diamonds it adds nodes for the Worlds and segments for the connections between them, and the diamonds come to represent “the product spaces.” “The ever-expanding explored territory is always surrounded by unexplored territory” (sign_new_star).
  8. c. 2480The Order’s 250th anniversary. On the very day ground is broken for this Warehouse, Lemur and the Director announce something “new and big” for the next facility: what will become the Tetratetrad (audiolog_sky_combiners_2). About ten years of work on this Warehouse follow.
  9. c. 2490sThe “today” of the conversations: some seventy thousand Warehouses have been built “across these dozen decades” (apparently counting from 2372), the decay is eating them, and new ones can’t be built inference

The end of Earth and the “Lifeboat societies”

The Acting Director tells this without pathos (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_1–3, audiolog_sky_wool). “It’s much easier to break things than to build them.” Technology changed society “change, after change, after change,” and people became “incredibly rich, but almost every way we employ these riches becomes deleterious to our well-being.” She compares them to “the wealthy children of some businessman”: bored, believing “they’re too smart to believe in meaning,” seeking reprieve in drugs, and then toying with weapons out of rage at their own emptiness. Once technology let a single lunatic set off a bomb or unleash a disease “just to see a million souls squirm and die,” Earth was doomed.

Salvation came from the infant technique of creating space: “Create space, tweak its rules, move into the new house, close the door behind,” reopening it “just a crack, once in a while, for carefully-scheduled rendezvous.” The planet “was soon gone.” Humanity now lives “Wherever”: “Zoom out, and it’s spatial steel wool,” and even the well-known parts are hard to map. The Director recalls the “upheavals and calamities that we were lucky to survive — seemed lucky to survive”; a note explains this isn’t bitterness but a hint that something like divine intervention may have helped (audiolog_u_games_inspirations).

The refugees formed “Lifeboat societies.” Nature had held mankind in check through its very harshness; “In mastering Nature, we removed the constraints. No more container. A turtle without its shell.” The shell had to be replaced. Societies with arbitrary rules dissolved: “if everyone knows the rules are arbitrary, why not break them, if it feels good in the moment?” Her own society didn’t survive either. The Order survived through belief (“a reason to be that transcends human whim”) and ambition. Members aren’t required to believe anything specific, but the Order’s structure was created according to these beliefs: “‘It’s in the water’, as they say. (The water that we drink, that is.)” Of the other societies she concludes, in Kurt Vonnegut’s words: “So it goes.” Belief itself she explains pragmatically: beliefs “have survival value.” Leamington later tells the Director: “Your Acting said this is a survival tactic... But it seems you really believe it” (audiolog_u_chaos_1; more in chapter XII).

From combiners to the Tetratetrad

The earliest Warehouses mixed individual objects and behaviors; beyond a certain size “the outcomes tended towards inscrutability.” So the Order began borrowing several elements from a single world at once and combining worlds, the very argument Blow makes for his own four (chapter I): first “2-combiners,” then 3-, and finally 4-combiners, whose cross “splays itself nicely across a two-dimensional map” and “celebrates the dimensional axes” (audiolog_sky_combiners–4). Ours is “one of the more audacious of the 4-combiners.”

For the Order’s 250th anniversary, with the Warehouses’ serial numbers “about to reach an arcane and auspicious number” (an inside joke understood only by Lemur, the Director “and some other Highests”), they decided to build something enormous. Lemur, the Founder, found a 5-combiner “Too small” with “poor symmetry!” A detail in passing: the “First Founder,” under whom the Order arose in 2230, is still personally arguing about the shape of the next Warehouse a quarter of a millennium later. People in the Order live a very long time, and that prospect of “greatly prolonging my own life” is exactly what Leamington calls “quite a temptation” inference

A 6-combiner, spread along three axes, was rejected too: “one or two hundred” Implementors could build it, and they wanted something “so audacious, it required all hands.” An 8-combiner “barbell” of two crosses was put forward by “another of the Highest Implementors,” and a note gives the game away: “She is the Highest Implement[or] of whom she is speaking here” (audiolog_sky_combiners_4). Lemur liked the thinking and found it half-hearted: “why stop there?” They settled on “a 4-combiner of 4-combiners”: four Warehouses the size of this one, mixed by the same cross “writ large” (audiolog_sky_tetratetrad). The Director called it “our own small Leviathan.” So the Acting Director tells the story of the great leap with herself left out of it, though the leap that would end in the decay began as her proposal before the Founder pushed it further inference Models of these forms stand on the upper tier, labeled “A 6-combiner,” “Barbell” and “Tetratetrad” (sign_combiners_3, sign_combiners_4, sign_tetratetrad).

The project revived a fading Order: “many souls we’d not seen in decades re-emerged from obscurity, smiling wide.” The Director tells the other side of it (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1–3). The worlds inside each tetrad “were chosen for maximum density”; the team “kept Tetrising the tetrads,” swapping worlds and keeping “a farm league of candidate tetrads.” Years of work on a world could end with “Poof”; very beautiful puzzles “were regularly dropped into Cold Storage”; and Implementors were consoled that “the real product here is the refinement of your soul.” The Tetratetrad was to be “the brightest star in our crown. Or it would be, when finished.” And then the Order hit a wall: “the Holographic Principle still held surprises.”

Ten years on this Warehouse, a “farm league” of spare worlds, discarded puzzles, consolation about “the refinement of your soul”: this sounds very much like Blow’s descriptions of making the game itself. It began as “a small, year-and-a-half project” to prove out his new programming language and took ten years; he expected to finish faster than The Witness and realized in year five that he was about halfway done; and he now says, as the Acting Director might of her last Warehouse, “I can’t do another ten-year game” (Wookash Podcast). His first plan had six games; the Order turns down a 6-combiner as too small. Not everything is autobiography, though. In life the four source games were always first on the list and none was ever swapped out, so the Tetratetrad’s endless “Tetrising” is fiction, not chronicle. And the “arcane and auspicious” serial number has a strong candidate: 69,105, the number of leaves in Zork (chapter XI).

The decay

The decay “is eating into our Warehouses as though they were Swiss cheese” (audiolog_u_decay_1). The Director explains it this way. “Classical holography has an information density limit, that you measure in Planck-area units,” but for “causally heterogeneous spaces,” joined by connective arcs, “we face a tighter constraint,” “a kind of super-information, or super-causation” (audiolog_u_decay_3). “Some think the bounds involve consciousness in some way,” which he finds possible, “given the way our projections interact with awareness via the Hook channels.” “Expanding the volume of the space doesn’t help us any more.” Ours is “one of the worst” Warehouses.

What strikes the Director is the contrast: “It’s so funny. In our physical Ensemble, look at the incredible amount of stuff that exists without any trouble: all those galaxies you see when looking into the sky, and all the incredible space beyond” (audiolog_u_decay_4).

However God built the ceiling of that cathedral is still a mystery; our Order’s ceiling is lower, and lacks vaulting.

The Director, audiolog_u_decay_4
  • Isolation. Any connection with an outside party makes it “part of our causal cluster,” and “the increased density of the enlarged cluster accelerates the decay.” So “the creations of the Static and Dynamic Brigades are firewalled from each other,” and some people “went off to start a disjoint colony sending only rare and cryptic communications — a low-density restart” (audiolog_u_decay_4).
  • Fresh eyes. They have “only just realized that fresh observers help with preservation”: “those who have never before experienced a particular Warehouse, when it’s all new to them,” somehow “strengthen the projections for a while” (audiolog_u_decay_3). “So you, unexpected Visitor, have helped us a bit already,” the Director tells Leamington, “and there are a few more Warehouses I’d like you to visit...” Freshness here means a first visit, not innocence: an experienced visitor stays useful wherever he has not yet been.
  • Burning is no answer. Some want to destroy older Warehouses to make room for new ones. The Director objects: even reduced to ash, specks of dust look interchangeable to humans, “but God knows the history and personal character of each and every one.” “The amount of information never goes down. All that happens is, when you burn things, your personal ignorance goes up. The apparent distance between you and God grows” (audiolog_u_decay_5).
  • Building is no answer either. In his last recording, Leamington hopes someday to design his own Warehouse, but first there’s “a problem they must solve that lies beyond my present learning” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_5). The Director told him that erosion comes slowly at first, then accelerates, “and before you can blink, it’s a landslide.” “But if decay can come quickly, perhaps it can go quickly.”

How the Warehouse works from the inside

No single recording explains the Warehouse’s machinery, but the scattered details add up to a coherent picture inference

  • It is made of projections. The Director calls the Order’s spaces “our projections”: they “interact with awareness via the Hook channels,” and fresh observers “strengthen the projections for a while” (audiolog_u_decay_3). In the Order’s physics a volume is fully set by the information on its boundary (audiolog_u_boundary_matching_1), and simply “Expanding the volume of the space doesn’t help us any more.”
  • The people in the worlds aren’t quite people. Meeting the Acting Director, Leamington says: “You are the first person I have seen in ... quite some time. The first person in person, I mean, not as a ... figment? What do you call it” (audiolog_sky_take_a_tour). Earlier he wondered why he had yet to see “a real person here, not in the worlds” (audiolog_g_work_and_care). He seems to count the heroes of the worlds as something like figments.
  • The Visitor steers other people’s bodies. “I feel as though I’m making the decisions ... but I’m sure they believe the decisions are theirs” (audiolog_g_possession). The Queen, passing through a beam herself for the first time, notices the difference: per the note, this time it is “happening to me,” “as opposed to one of the characters in the worlds that she drops into and plays” (queen_og_first_green_beam).
  • The Warehouse resets. A guest’s recordings “would be considered foreign objects and would be automatically removed upon your exit” (audiolog_s_tradition_of_our_people_part_2b). Leamington replays this Warehouse “after every third” (end1_dimension_3), and before the first ending he finishes a “reconnection” while the Visitor waits “but a handful of seconds” (end1_dimension_0). Inside the pockets you can undo and restart; on the Overworld there is “No Restart” (no_restart_on_the_overworld).
  • The Order can make solid copies that answer back. Leamington’s hologram in the third ending is solid to the touch, draws “upon a wider variety of pre-written responses,” and serves as “a somewhat more-personal substitute for my presence” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_1).
  • And yet the heroes remember. Two years later, per the note, “Everyone has done all these puzzles and gone back home,” and once back they recognize the Warehouse: “Hey ... We did this already!” (all_together_now_reassembly_1). Isidore recognizes a near-identical level (sp_gem_transport_2), and Tif asks point-blank whether she is looking at “a duplicate, timber for timber” (hp_endpoint_weapon_mid2).
  • It remembers coarsely. The undo history is kept at eight levels of resolution: the last few hundred moves one by one, older ones merged in blocks of 25, then 625, then 15,625. When a block is merged, only each thing’s state at the start and at the end of the block survives, and anything created and destroyed inside it leaves no trace code It is the Director’s holography applied to time: “What matters is the aggregate value” (audiolog_u_boundary_matching_2) hypothesis And undo never brings back the fog of war: you can take back a move, but not what you saw while making it code

hypothesisLive possession, or a cast

The most economical reading: the Visitor isn’t walking through living people in real time, but through responsive projections, casts of heroes who once walked this road themselves. A cast answers the way its original would, and the Visitor inhabits it through the same channels that link projections to awareness. That would explain why Leamington sees the heroes as “figments” and why Tif suspects a copy. The main question stays open: did the original feel the Visitor’s hand at the moment it was being steered? Tif, for one, senses that “Someone creepy cares about me...” Proper time in different spaces isn’t correlated, so both versions may be true at once.

Why no one may speak to the Visitor

In the first ending the Acting Director reminds Leamington of “the new rule that we mustn’t speak directly with any Visitor of insufficient rank, regardless of any upset this may cause.” He asks whether it’s on his account. “I did enjoy our conversations, but they caused a kerfuffle.” And the rule is not even new: “It was supposed to have been the rule already” (end1_dimension_0). She is the one who broke it. Leamington explains it to the Queen himself: “We are not supposed to pollute you, you see” (end2_sokoban_writing_choice_1).

Put this together with the Director’s findings and the rule looks less like bureaucracy and more like a survival technique inference The Warehouse lives on first visits: a newcomer’s activity strengthens the projections, while every unnecessary contact densifies the causal cluster and speeds the decay. Telling a Visitor what lies ahead would spend the very thing the Order needs. The rule against speaking is an anti-spoiler policy with cosmic stakes, and the Order makes no secret of wanting more newcomers: “It’s quite nice having unexpected Visitors… I wouldn’t mind more of it,” the Acting Director says (end1_dimension_1). Hence Leamington’s brevity in the second ending, “to give space for the Puzzles themselves to speak.” The phrase is nearly Blow’s own: dialogue running over a puzzle, he says, “drowns out the puzzle’s ability to speak,” and his games refuse to tell players how to feel so that they can “have their own reaction” (livestream with Edmund McMillen; Wookash Podcast).

VIII

Three endings and a coda

The game ends three times, and each ending is hidden deeper than the last. First the Visitor is shown how the Warehouse is built, then allowed to intervene in other people’s levels, and finally taught to design them.

Blow has said that after the first ending the player should feel the game is genuinely finished, not that they were let go after playing a part of it; that is why the continuation is hidden, so that no one feels obliged to pursue it (Gamereactor interview). In his July 2026 talk in China he called it design hygiene: a player should see clearly where the main game ends and where the strange, hardcore extra material for the most devoted begins. The sign before the second ending says so itself: “You could have left this place much earlier; God knows most people do, but you kept on” (sign_end2_intro_2). The first ending: the Appreciation Ceremony

The Queen hears voices from above. They belong to the Acting Director and Leamington, who has just “performed the reconnection,” repairs after the decay, so well that “our Visitor hasn’t been waiting but a handful of seconds.” The Queen shouts at them, demands an answer, asks where they are speaking from. They don’t reply: that’s the new rule. Then the Acting Director, as “the seniormost present officer of the Order of the Sinking Star,” declares the Appreciation Ceremony “commenced. Free the zeroth block!” From there they take turns reciting a ritual text, and each stanza ends with the command “unfreeze”: one more block is freed from the ice.

In the code this is meant literally code The first ending is a level of its own with four world buttons: Heroes, Mirrors, Stones, Beams. Rocks set on the buttons choose a combination of worlds, and a puzzle room appears for that combination. There is always one rock per world the current stage combines: one at first, for four single-world rooms; then two, for six pairs; three, for four triples; and four, for the one room with everything: fifteen rooms in all. Once a stage is cleared, the next stanza plays and another rock comes out of the ice; after the last, the exit opens. The way here: activate four of the six ground towers, open four gold rooms at the Antipode with their teleporters, and the sign at the entrance sets it free.

The ceremony closes with “So mote it be,” the old ritual ending of Masonic rites. Between the stanzas they chat. Leamington admits that watching an Endgame “from this perspective” is “exhilarating, really,” but “I don’t understand all the ancillary data I’m seeing.” “Nobody understands all of it!” The joke has a literal backing: the campaign save keeps ten counters, from gems picked up to friendly-dragon toggles, which the game writes on every save and never reads, and one of them is never even incremented code He replays this Warehouse “after every third” other one; the Acting Director warns that “eventually, you’ll find that too much. The third stanza names the figure outright: “A pyramid standing before the dawn of time, its volume hides mysteries” (end1_dimension_3). Assembling them is the Overworld’s own four-world puzzle. The third ending: a game design course from a hologram

The third ending is led by Leamington’s “hologram.” Where he comes from, the word “indicates a mirage made only of light,” but this hologram is solid to the touch (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_1). He could have voiced its responses, but deliberately kept them in writing. Trying different possibilities, the player would hear the same response again and again, and “must stand there listening awhile, merely to discover whether the response has changed!”; a Sign “can be inspected in an instant; and, it’s no surprise when a Sign doesn’t change” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_2). It is an exam for him too: to author the hologram’s responses he had to learn to set down “a sequence of instructions to be performed by an automatic device,” which, as the sequence grows longer, “begins to exhibit behaviors you did not foresee” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_4).

This ending is hidden deepest of all, literally code There is no way down from the ground. The shaft at the Crossroads runs straight from the sky to the underworld, but on the ground floor it is fenced off on all four sides, and its platform waits at the top under a plug of three objects: a plain rock, a crystal and a skipping stone. Called down, the stack weighs three, more than a single push can move, so it can only be taken apart from above, by pulling or swapping. To go down, you first have to go up. Arriving on the lower tier earns the rank of Intrepid. The course itself runs through Exit Low: eight rooms in a chain, each opened by the exit of the one before: heroes, design, exploits, simplification, mirrors, off-topic and order, environment art, and the pipeline. The first room holds the tasks about the Queen’s own climb (below), and the last level of the pipeline opens a back door from the final room into the first, so the course physically closes into a loop. The pipeline earns the Visitor the highest rank, Sokoban.

The judge’s responses add up to a real textbook. A few of its rules:

  • “A pattern is strongest if you establish it before breaking it”: an exception belongs at the end of a sequence, “as a grace note” (judge_end3_reorder_mirror_4).
  • An emitter is more interesting when it is needed more than once; the best version of a level uses “every Beam” twice (judge_end3_redundant_beams_1).
  • You know you’ve found an exploit “when a major terrain feature is not used, objects aren’t used, or the method of solving the puzzle changes” (judge_fix_exploit1_2).
  • A rock deliberately placed “on the wrong parity” can hold up “a small idea”: a second place from which to try teleporting to the exit, and that place is a trap (judge_simplify_wizard_5).
  • Gold and ruby materials are “special reserved” and not for ordinary objects; repeated rocks need “some subtle variation”; the ocean grid “should be visible in areas where it helps players count and align squares,” and not where it misleads or distracts (judge_environment_art_*).
  • “Jailbreaks” are loopholes that let you carry an object out of a level into the Overworld, where it could wreak havoc. Having patched them, the judge admits: “It’s hard to be fully confident we found them all, though... there are a lot of moving parts” (judge_wizard_jailbreak_correct).
  • “Eliminate any possibility of clones diverging, even, especially, if a malicious player wants to give you grief” (judge_end2_clone_divergence_1).
  • Where to add a single square of land to introduce a newcomer to the Mirror without spoiling anything. The judge goes through every option: an extra islet distracts, and “creating attention-pulling features that do not contribute to the solution is poor workmanship” (judge_end2_mirror1_5).

IX

Philosophy and science

The conversations of the Acting Director and the Director with Leamington are the game’s philosophical frame. Three sources converge on one idea: the universe generates forms by itself, and a puzzle is a way of seeing it happen. These are the characters’ convictions, not theorems. As Blow puts it, a fictional character’s view “doesn’t even have to be exactly right”; the picture comes from setting several such views side by side.

The Play of Form and the “Static Līlā”

The Order, the Acting Director says, “was founded from the meeting of two old ‘spiritual’ traditions, together with a newer one that’s more like your own Natural Philosophy” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_1). The old traditions share the idea “that the Universe is engaged in a Play of Form; a constant, effortless action, performed out of the sheer fullness of being”: a child becomes an adult, a storm whips through the trees, one nation goes to war with another. Science adds the view from inside Time: law is “a set of rules, not unlike the rules of a game,” and rules repeated over and over are physics, chemistry, the spread of disease. “When the rules are chosen just right, they generate. They create consequences that are bountiful and sometimes deeply surprising.”

“When a Mirror and a Skipping Stone come together,” the Order sees “a specific instance of the Universe’s ever-unfolding process of creation.” It sifts the explosion of possibility for the most delightful results and frames them in puzzles: “That’s the why of all these puzzles” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_1a). The sign at the entrance names the brigade: “Static Līlā Brigade” (sign_forged), and līlā is Sanskrit for divine play. From the Order’s practice: meditate on what you have seen, play “another ten Warehouses, another hundred,” then learn to “expand it outward,” meeting the world with what you know, eyes open, walking around, “even when speaking with someone” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_1b). The note under that line is a quiet joke on Leamington: “This is a very meta line because she’s doing it right now.”

Which two old traditions? The game names neither, but leaves both in plain sight inference One is the Dao, whose book the Acting Director quotes a few talks later (below). The other is named on the brigade’s sign: līlā is the Indian idea, shared by Vaishnava and Shaiva theology, that the world is the divine at play, made not out of need but out of fullness; “the sheer fullness of being” is almost a translation.

Spencer-Brown’s “Laws of Form”

The third canonical text is George Spencer-Brown’s mathematical book Laws of Form (1969), though the game never names the author. Spencer-Brown himself tied it to the Dao: the book opens with an untranslated line from the first chapter of the Tao Te Ching, on the nameless as the beginning of heaven and earth, so the Acting Director’s pairing is his. For “One” to appear there must be a Distinction: a line separating A from Not A (audiolog_sky_laws_of_form). Read as rules of play, the two laws describe a level inference The mark is both a boundary (a wall, a shore, the plane of a mirror) and the act of crossing it. Crossing twice is the same as not crossing: step through a mirror and back, swap places with the Wizard twice, go out and back in through a wardrobe door, and nothing has changed. A puzzle is what happens when something from another rule is slipped in between the two crossings, a pushed stone, a turned beam, a clone, so that the way back no longer cancels out. The “countably infinite jungle” of forms grows exactly there. And the Acting Director’s “the Dao identifying itself” (audiolog_sky_10000_things) has a twin at the end of Laws of Form, where a world that wants to see itself must first split into a seer and a seen.

Complexification: where the new comes from

The most practical conversation explains why worlds are combined at all (audiolog_sky_complexification_1–2). A Mirror teleports objects “to equidistant positions”; a Skipping Stone travels in one direction. Bring them together and the Mirror acts “not just on the Stone’s position, but this direction as well.” “What if the new direction happens to be vertical, the one axis affected by Gravity? Let’s find out.” And it works both ways: “The Stone can make some things happen to the Mirror as well.”

She does the arithmetic by hand. One world has a Thief and a Goblin: one line. Another has Gems and “a Beam That Lets You Walk Through Walls”: another line. Put all four objects in one place: the old pairs stay as they were, but any two objects from different worlds “want to play together now.” Two lines become six, “without adding any new objects.” “Reality is always combining the elements we already have and producing things never before seen.” “In the limit it’s 2 to the nth power, where n is the number of ideas across all the worlds” (audiolog_sky_combiners). Strictly, 2n counts every subset of a set of ideas, the empty one included: a ceiling on combinations, not a count of puzzles, while pairs grow far more slowly. Why that growth stays learnable at all is the other half of the design (chapter I).

Surprise

What makes a puzzle valuable? “A primary indicator of interest is surprise”: the puzzle seemed mysterious or impossible, “but then BAM, with an insight, you see the answer,” and your mind “now holds something special that it didn’t before” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2_surprise). A random assortment doesn’t surprise, “there’s nothing delightful about a box of junk,” and random things “feel the same as each other.” An arrangement that is “too spare, too flat” doesn’t surprise either: “you can see everything about it on the first look.” The composition “needs to be on the edge of the Visitor’s understanding,” and real enjoyment comes when the surprise is not arbitrary, “like which direction to go in a maze,” but “reveals something salient about the world you’re in.” “This particular fact is scarcely taken seriously outside our Order.”

Then comes a debate (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_3). Leamington explains the pleasure of surprise in Darwinian terms: surprise signals “something wrong or incomplete in one’s understanding of the world,” positive emotion anchors the repair in memory, and “creatures with positive feelings about surprise die less, and reproduce more.” The Acting Director doesn’t argue, but goes further: “Perhaps the Universe is engaged in a constant process of generating its own delight,” and when we solve a puzzle we are “harmonizing with it.” “Perhaps strengthening that connection is a skill we can learn.” “Well. I am here to listen.”

hypothesisSurprise as camatkāra

Indian aesthetics has a word for the Order’s highest reward. In Abhinavagupta’s theory of art, camatkāra is the flash of astonished delight in which the self tastes its own consciousness, the point where aesthetic and spiritual experience meet. The Acting Director’s “BAM, with an insight,” and her guess that our joy in a solved puzzle is “tapping into that background delight, harmonizing with it” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_3), are close cousins of the idea, and the word would sit naturally beside līlā on the brigade’s sign. The game never uses it.

Chaos, compression and cipher

Leamington doesn’t understand how anyone can believe in a beneficent God when “immense and deadly storms” sweep away whole towns and cells of the body “randomly become corrupted, and turn upon the whole” (audiolog_u_chaos_1). The Director answers with information theory (audiolog_u_chaos_2). A message can be compressed: write “armch” instead of “armchair,” replace a frequent “it’s a lovely morning” with “kzq.” Near the limit, improving the compression “requires a tremendous amount of effort — one might say a Godly amount of effort,” and the perfectly compressed result “appears as completely random noise.” So does a well-enciphered message. They can’t be told apart, from each other or from true randomness.

So. Any time that life looks scrambled, or chaotic, or meaningless... It may just be that you lack the decipherment key.

The Director, audiolog_u_chaos_2

It is a direct metaphor for a puzzle: until you find the solution, a level looks arbitrary; after the insight, it looks inevitable inference One more small thing: the Director asks Leamington to “push the button on your recorder there, so that you get down these words in my native language,” to look them up later in the Order’s library. The conversations run through telepathic translation, and precision needs the original words.

Mathematics backs one half of the Director’s “You can’t know,” and the half matters inference Algorithmic information theory measures a string by the length of the shortest program that produces it, its Kolmogorov complexity. A perfectly compressed message has no pattern left in it, or it could be squeezed further: its meaning lives entirely in the decoder. An enciphered message is the opposite case. In principle it is no more complex than its plaintext plus a short key, but finding that out is computationally hopeless, so it passes for noise. Either way, Gregory Chaitin showed that no consistent formal system can certify a string as random beyond a fixed bound: you can never prove that a long stretch of chaos is only noise. That is all the theorem gives. It rules out a proof of meaninglessness, not meaninglessness itself, and it says nothing about whether a hidden message would be kind. The step from “no one can prove it is noise” to “it is a message from a beneficent God” is the Director’s, not mathematics’.

The third ending’s course runs the same idea in reverse. Cutting stray rocks, ragged shores and redundant emitters strips a level toward the shortest statement of its idea, and the result is the opposite of a cipher: the cleaner the level, the louder its structure, which is exactly why the critic finds he can “read” it (chapter XII). A designer who wants to be understood removes noise. The God of the Director’s lecture, if he exists, writes in cipher.

The Order’s physics

  • Boundary swapping. “The universe acts holographically”: “everything inside a volume of space is determined by some information at the boundary.” If you change the boundary of your own space to match the boundary of the place you want to go, “then you can swap the two boundaries and all contents therein.” The energy “is proportional to the surface area,” and for smaller spaces it’s “not really so much” (audiolog_u_boundary_matching_1).
  • How to find a world you know nothing about. Guess the boundary of a tiny volume that fits “just a few particles,” fail until a swap succeeds; those particles become “an initial sensor”; use their data to try a slightly bigger boundary, “perturbing things a little, at random,” and repeat until you fit inside the swapped space yourself. “Famously, you can’t clone a quantum state, but here you don’t need to, because a bunch of stuff cancels out.” “One might even think it was designed this way” (audiolog_u_boundary_matching_2). It answers a question Leamington asked at the very start: by his people’s way of doorsteading the search would be random, “exposing the traveler to unknown hazards each time” (audiolog_g_world_selection_2). The search is indeed random, but what it risks is a few particles, not people inference
  • The ash and the black hole. The Order’s holography, the Director says, was “first gleaned from the study of black holes” (audiolog_u_boundary_matching_1), and the game borrows from that physics with care inference In the real theory, the information a region can hold is bounded by the area of its boundary, not by its volume (the holographic bound that grew out of Bekenstein’s and Hawking’s work on black holes). That alone would not make bigger spaces useless, since a bigger volume has a bigger boundary; the game adds a law of its own. For “causally heterogeneous spaces” the Director meets “a tighter constraint,” and only under it does “Expanding the volume of the space” stop helping (audiolog_u_decay_3). Real physics supplies the vocabulary; the Order’s predicament is fiction. His argument against burning old Warehouses is the black-hole information debate in miniature. Quantum evolution is unitary: information is never destroyed, only scrambled into correlations no one can read, and in the information-theoretic reading of thermodynamics (E. T. Jaynes) entropy measures what the observer doesn’t know, not anything missing from the world. “The amount of information never goes down. All that happens is, when you burn things, your personal ignorance goes up” is close to a textbook sentence. In a game whose undo button reassembles every smashed rock and every drowned hero, it is also a design rule, with one telling exception: undo restores the board, never the fog of war, so what the player has seen stays seen (chapter VII).
  • Raining fish. The whole field began with “historical cases where it had been raining fish,” confirmed “including by reliable observers,” the kind of reports Charles Fort used to collect. One man invented boundary swapping to explain them, spent years scaling it up to “human-sized spaces,” and in the end “realized that boundary swapping couldn’t explain the fish”: the boundary conditions “would just have been too different; unmatchable.” Since “the Earth as we knew it is now long gone,” the mystery will stay unsolved.
  • The “Ultimate Ensemble.” The Director’s “primary task” is to “discover what we don’t yet understand about the laws of the Ultimate Ensemble, and the related holography” (audiolog_u_decay_2). The term belongs to the cosmologist Max Tegmark: the multiverse of all mathematical structures, where every consistent set of rules exists somewhere as a world. That is where the Order “sources” its worlds.
  • Bulks, orthogonality, proper time. A “bulk” is the physicists’ term for a higher-dimensional space containing our spacetime. The “temporal orthogonality” of 2276 is the moment when time in different spaces stopped coinciding. “Proper time,” in the director’s notes, is the time lived inside one’s own space: with causal isolation it isn’t tied to anything else. Hence Reynard’s fourteen years, Leamington’s sixteen months and the Queen’s ten seconds.

The cement between worlds

To Leamington’s questions about the “substrate,” the Director gives a practical answer: when worlds merge, “they don’t connect to each other automatically. We must add cement between,” and “choosing the composition of that cement is our opportunity to exert authorial voice” (audiolog_u_wizard_mirror_worlds_connect). For example: how should the Wizard interact with the Mirror? You could treat the Mirror as “any old Stone” that he swaps with: “Simple. Clean. But boring, and irresponsible.” The Mirror has a shape, it is “reflective in two directions”; what should that mean for the Wizard? The decision may create “new, additional vortices of behavior,” or players may find it “dull, boring, contrived.” “Would you have done it differently? ... Sometimes you’d be right. But you must always really think it through.”

This is Blow’s own method, nearly word for word, down to the example. “If I have the wizard and the magic mirror, what could be interesting about those?” he said in an interview in China: the two come from different worlds, so the team had to add rules, and “it’s important how we choose those, because we could create more life in terms of more possibility, or we could create like deadness where not a lot can happen.” Those choices were made early and then frozen: “once you’ve figured out what the good version of the rules is, we really don’t change it or mess with it,” and from then on no exceptions and no hidden “if statements” to force a scene (Wookash Podcast). The designers choose the cement. What the cement then contains is not theirs to choose.

Computable beauty

The Director shows Leamington the Mandelbrot set on a handheld device (audiolog_u_computation). Leamington recognizes “the Clayson’s Field,” which he has only seen “by still images in books and papers,” and sees it move smoothly for the first time. The rule is elementary: “Square it, add the two-dimensional constant for that position in the plane, and that’s the new number, and repeat.” The lesson to absorb “over and over”: “the math is so simple, obviously we don’t put the complexity in there, ourselves. That complexity and structure is just already there somehow.” Then the Director leaves Leamington Conway’s “Life”: “Experiment; see what you can figure out.”

The name matters too inference Leamington’s world had no computers, yet someone there, presumably a Clayson, found the same infinitely intricate shape, and his culture named it after him, as ours named it after Benoit Mandelbrot. Two humanities, two names, one object: the plainest argument in the whole game that the shape was “already there” before anyone looked (on the Platonism behind it, see chapter XII).

Old games and audio logs

The Order’s games grew out of the games of its ancestors: “cards, or little wooden men,” then “very special gears, spinning at billions of revolutions per second,” which is how the Director describes logic circuits to a man from a world without computers. Leamington is horrified: “The size, the immense heat, and the noise! Would they not ignite the very air?” “These gears are very small, you could neither hear nor see them.” Then came “video” screens, “and thus the medium of ‘video games’ was born, and flourished for a while” (audiolog_u_old_games). The Order sometimes goes back to them, “to return to a childlike state, to try and notice what we’ve lost in the modern day. And sometimes, we do find little nuggets of gold” (audiolog_u_games_inspirations). The game itself is assembled from such nuggets: free puzzle games made in PuzzleScript, and Promesst.

The Acting Director also explains the tradition of audio logs: in the old entertainments, you came upon “a narrated recording left for you by some fictional character,” then another, then another (audiolog_s_tradition_of_our_people_part_1). Later the Order mostly stopped making them: after hundreds of works it’s hard to keep finding new things to say, and repetition “diminishes the work.” Then she explains how she will place this very conversation: the first part “in an easily-accessible place,” the second “in a location that must be deliberately earned, but that is plainly visible on first entry”; and a short recording shouldn’t go in a hard-to-reach place, “We mustn’t disappoint!”, but in an easy one, where “it will feel like a small gift” for reaching a new area (..._part_2, ..._part_3). It is a rare case of a game explaining out loud why its own logs are where they are. The critic Matthewmatosis notes that the other recordings, too, seem to stand next to puzzles on the same subject (chapter XII).

The revisions hall: Blow’s lessons

A separate hall, “Design Illustrations,” shows old and new versions of levels with commentary (audiolog_di_*, 25 recordings). In the subtitle file this voice carries the same director tag as the Director-on-Sabbatical, but this is a real account of the development: it speaks of “Jonah’s original design,” “Zach’s original version,” “Patrick’s revision.” In the game’s data, voice recordings in fact have four possible authors: SOKOBAN (Leamington), ACTING (the Acting Director), SABBATICAL (the Director-on-Sabbatical) and a separate DEVELOPER, and there is a special flag for recordings heard only in this hall code Most likely it is Blow himself inference The main points:

  • Difficulty can be good or bad. When a level gives no foothold at first sight, and a long game has many such levels, the player relives the same experience over and over, “the experience of being stuck and confused.” And fatigue isn’t the worst part: if an hour goes to random attempts and only the last five minutes to understanding, “your thoughtspace is cluttered,” and the ideas the level was made for are lost (audiolog_di_catching_up_v0_part_2).
  • Difficulty has to be on-topic. Blow’s first rule, outside the hall: “the puzzle has to give you back more than it asks of you,” and “we don’t make it hard unless the reason why it’s hard is part of the idea of the level.” Early on the team tried to “maximize the pushing” in every level; the result was “way too hard, tedious, and less fun,” so it went (Wookash Podcast).
  • Context decides. Yet the original version of that same level isn’t declared a mistake: “What’s better or worse is always contextual,” and in a short game it might beat the revision, since the most memorable moments come when a challenge looks impossible: “Then you notice one little thing, and it breaks the whole puzzle open” (audiolog_di_catching_up_v0).
  • A song slowed down twenty times. Slow your favorite song down by a factor of two and “the tempo is off, the pitches of the notes are weird”; by a factor of twenty, “it doesn’t even work as music any more,” just “weird low vibrations.” The ideas puzzles carry also have “a particular range of time scales,” and a game where each level takes too long breaks the melody (audiolog_di_catching_up_v2).
  • “Design Elegance Goggles.” Through them, the northwest area of Jonah Ostroff’s level “looks pretty awkward; it isn’t really used, except to suggest the continuation of the grid” (audiolog_di_heroes_1_7). The revision turns the level into “an illustration of the Thief’s movement patterns”: she starts right next to the button, while the crystal has to be dragged back through every room. The same idea, “It’s interesting and funny when the Thief has to pull a Crystal circuitously,” later becomes a task in the third ending (judge_end3_heroes_1_20).
  • Purity of character. If a level doesn’t care how things are moved, use a hero who can only push: “pushing one thing in this game is ‘the default idea’ and introduces the least noise.” On Zach’s level: “We don’t know why Zach made this a Thief level” (audiolog_di_across_the_fence_old).
  • Less clutter. The original Skipping Stones “was often about solving the subtle maze” of islands, and here the team deliberately “traveled away from the maze aesthetic”: “maze-like clutter in the geometry of islands” reduces clarity (sign_simplify_skipping_0–3). Remove water, skipping and lilies if the level is about something else; “Instead of 5 monsters, we cut it down to 3”; give a stone an “on-topic” job; ask whether each subtask could be done “in a cleaner way” (audiolog_di_height_chooser_v0, audiolog_di_worlds_collide_heroes_and_promesst_v3).
  • A game, not homework. In a level that is too cramped you keep dying in the dragon’s breath, and a hard final trick “can become much larger in your memory than the thing we wanted you to remember” (audiolog_di_torcher). But a version that is too easy fails too: “Okay, I did the steps you told me to, therefore I won the level. So what?” “This is a game, not a homework assignment” (audiolog_di_quad_torch_v1).
  • A level asks a question. A vertical Gate “with no apparent function out in the middle of the floor” is a good question: “obviously you are supposed to do something with that Gate, but what?” Using every object for several purposes is not always mastery; sometimes it “just fogs up the core idea” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified). Worst of all is a level that “is actively misleading you,” suggesting a move familiar from other levels that is useless here (audiolog_di_strategic_stack).
  • Readable, not given away. A version that is “now very hard to read” is bad (audiolog_di_sender_simplified), and so is a Goblin in a “suspicious position” that is no part of the solution (audiolog_di_sender). But remove all the confusion and “maybe we are closer to just giving the player the answer,” so the final version keeps its Gate “slightly hidden” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2). On the price of “reading the level,” see chapter XII.
  • The author knows the answer. “It’s easy to underestimate the difficulty, because you know the answer already, because you came up with it!” (audiolog_di_push_down_a_lot_small).
  • Humility. “It’s a little humbling to know that your puzzles could always be better,” and in a game of significant size it’s “very very hard to get anywhere close to the best versions of all the puzzles” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2).

Recurring motifs

Fewer facts here and more reading: which themes run through all five worlds and how they add up to a single idea. The connections are marked as inferences and hypotheses.

Tennyson’s “Ulysses” as the skeleton of the game

The title comes from “Ulysses,” and the game has four signs with its lines (sign_follow, sign_thought, sign_strive, sign_arch). One of them is about an arch: “Yet all experience is an arch wherethro’ / Gleams that untravell’d world whose margin fades / For ever and forever when I move.” The sign with the Order’s new emblem restates the same thought in its own words: “The ever-expanding explored territory is always surrounded by unexplored territory” (sign_new_star). The upper tier even has a zone called “Archways” inference

But the match goes beyond the title. Tennyson’s Ulysses is an aging king tired of meting and doling “unequal laws unto a savage race.” He finds it dull “to rust unburnish’d, not to shine in use”; he leaves the sceptre to his son Telemachus and puts out to sea one last time, since “’Tis not too late to seek a newer world.” This is an almost exact summary of Reynard inference The kingdom’s chief judge is weary of the routine of sentencing, fears his “time of youthful creativity” is “expended,” trains his son Ratleigh “to assume my duties,” and departs through a “special transport” into a new world. Tennyson has “I am a part of all that I have met”; Reynard has “now we’re all a part of something bigger.”

The same plot repeats in other storylines. The Trader believes he is dying and sets out on a last voyage, as if to do “some work of noble note” before the end, while his clone complains of an indifferent wife; Tennyson’s Ulysses is likewise “match’d with an aged wife.” The Acting Director, old and “artificially strengthened,” builds what turns out to be her last Warehouse and records her prayer again. The aging Order, whose “membership was dwindling,” comes back to life for the Tetratetrad: “you and I are old; / Old age hath yet his honour and his toil.”

And the poem has a reverse side, which the game keeps too inference Read without the heroic glow, Tennyson’s Ulysses is a man walking out on his duties. He calls his people “a savage race,” leaves an “aged wife,” and hands the dull work of governing to his son with faint praise: “He works his work, I mine.” Dante put Ulysses among the fraudulent counselors of the eighth circle of Hell and let him tell how his fine speech about virtue and knowledge led his crew past the edge of the world to drown. The game is full of such departures. Reynard leaves his slate of convictions to Ratleigh. The Trader’s clone dreams of leaving wife and child to become “itinerant.” The Director leaves the running of the Order to his deputy (“appoint a successor if you’re tired”), and she hands this Warehouse to Leamington, who keeps patching it. And the Order itself, chasing “the vast and endless sea,” builds denser and denser worlds until they rot. Following a sinking star is glorious; it is also following something that goes down.

Leamington’s motto is “Ever forward”; a note says it carries a hint of “Keep Calm and Carry On” (intro_before_teleporter). Valens says that “sometimes, the only way out is ... forward”; when the dragon thanks the Caver, he can only answer, bewildered, “I was just going forward?”; and the six heroes cheer each other on: “If we keep pushing forward, we’ll get out of this, like always.” In a game whose main action is pushing, this is almost literal. And the poem’s last line stands on a sign (sign_strive): “To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.”

hypothesisA Dantean layer

Tennyson’s source is Canto 26 of Dante’s Inferno, where Ulysses sails beyond the known world and perishes near the mountain of Purgatory. The Queen’s first question is whether this is the afterlife; the Warehouse is divided into lower, middle and upper tiers; the judges speak of “a long climb” and finding “her way to a higher place”; and at the summit a sign says: “From the top, gaze further upward” (sign_pyramid). Perhaps the Overworld is also built as a small ascent modeled on Dante’s mountain. There are no direct references to Dante in the files.

To justify the ways of the Maker

The Acting Director’s “invocation” is taken from the opening of Paradise Lost, ending with the purpose of the whole poem: “I may assert Eternal Providence, And justifie the wayes of God to men” (paradise_lost_invocation_2). The game keeps showing this from the other side, the designer’s side inference

  • The judges in the endings demand fairness to the character: “We must be fair,” “She needs a fair shot,” “We hold a high position. We must not use it for cruelty.” Whoever builds a world for someone else answers for their suffering.
  • Leamington asks Job’s question: how can one believe in a loving God when the world is so cruel? The Director answers with the cipher: chaos may be a message for which you lack the key. Anyone solving a puzzle knows this: until the insight, the level feels like a cruel joke.
  • The Director’s own “invocation” is chapter 41 of the Book of Job, the speech about Leviathan, in which God answers Job not with an explanation but with a vision of the grandeur of creation. And the Director called the Tetratetrad “our own small Leviathan.” The Order seems to answer the question of meaning with the same gesture: by building a spectacle.
  • Psalm 139, which the Acting Director reads, is about a God from whom there is no hiding: “If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; Even there shall thy hand lead me,” and “in thy book all my members were written, which in continuance were fashioned, when as yet there was none of them.” For a game in which the player’s hand leads heroes through levels built in advance, it is a very precise choice.

Lay the three invocations side by side and they read like three stages of a maker inference The Founder Lemur opened his Warehouses with Carroll’s Snark: play, nonsense, a hunt for something that may make you vanish. The Director chose Leviathan from Job 41, the terror and splendor of a made thing too great to master, and his reading stops on its last verse, “he is a king over all the children of pride” (audiolog_u_job_41_fragments). The Acting Director chose Milton’s prayer for light, “What in me is dark / Illumin,” and the purpose of justifying the ways of God. Play, power, humility. And the voice tagged jon, in the credits, repeats only the third.

hypothesisScales with no air between them

The verses the Director chose describe the Order’s own project with uncomfortable precision. “His scales are his pride, shut up together as with a close seal. One is so near to another, that no air can come between them”: a structure packed so tight that nothing can breathe, like the Tetratetrad, whose worlds were “chosen for maximum density” and whose decay begins where “the cross-correlation has become too dense.” “His breath kindleth coals, and a flame goeth out of his mouth”: a fire-breather, in a game full of fire-breathing dragons. “His heart is as firm as a stone”: in a game about pushing stones. And the reading ends on pride. If the Director chose these verses on purpose, his invocation is a warning addressed to the Order itself.

hypothesisA hook for Leviathan

The Director’s recording begins with the line “Canst thou draw out leviathan with an hook?” (audiolog_u_job_41_fragments). And the same Director explains the link between the Warehouses’ projections and consciousness through the “Hook channels” (audiolog_u_decay_3). If this isn’t a coincidence, the name of the channels answers the question God puts to Job: the hook with which the Order tries to draw out its “small Leviathan” is the attention of whoever solves the puzzles. The files never state this connection outright.

Who watches whom

Almost every character guides someone and is in turn watched by someone else. Lay out these relationships and you get a nested structure inference

From performer to maker

Almost every storyline traces the same arc: the piece on the board becomes the hand that moves the pieces inference

  • The Queen: from playing other people’s levels to designing them, her own path included.
  • Leamington: from explorer to Sokoban, author of signs and of a hologram, dreaming of his own Warehouse.
  • Reynard: from a judge who built traps for the heroes to a Sokoban of the Order and the author of the Queen’s rescue.
  • Ratleigh: from an ever-hushed spectator to heir to his father’s office.
  • The Trader: from the boy who failed his trial to an Apprentice who “will eventually” learn to make mirrors.
  • The Caver: from restorer of an old suit to builder of a second one.
  • Isidore: dreams of learning to create wrapping spaces, that is, of doing what the Order does.
  • The Dragon: from an egg to an emissary who will bring humans “new light.”
  • The Order itself: from a studio of computer simulations to builders of worlds.

A game about puzzles turns out to be a game about becoming someone who makes them. Hence the textbook third ending, the revisions hall, and, in the credits under “Design from Community and Friends,” the line “(Thanks, Chat!),” apparently addressed to the chat of Blow’s livestreams inference

Alongside it runs a motif of inheritance: from the Queen’s prayer as “heir to your charge” to the Warehouse the Acting Director calls “the first I will soon pass over to you” (more in chapter XII).

The skill is the flaw

Blow has handed over the key to the cast himself. The original Heroes of Sokoban turned character classes into puzzle abilities; making them people meant asking what kind of person moves like that, and his answer is that everyone in that world “is actually a little bit pathological”: a gift, plus a psychology that won’t let them use it in moderation (livestream with Edmund McMillen). The Thief can’t stop pulling, so she can pull herself into a dead end, and her weakness as a person is the same: “she brings too many things too close to her.” The Bard can’t let a charmed monster go without scraping it off against a wall, and she is “inherently manipulative,” having learned as a child to find “everybody’s special thing.” Edmund McMillen called the Wizard “compulsive,” and Blow agreed: with him it is “so easy to just like accidentally go across the map.”

The rule reaches past the North inference

  • Valens may not strike anyone, and the prohibition is the source of his strength: power squeezed through a restriction.
  • Isidore trades places with anything in sight: “great for moving heavy stuff, not so great for getting close to people.”
  • The Trader works by splitting into reflections and is whole only when they stand on one tile: a man who spent his life gluing himself back together.
  • The Caver’s suit lets him carry a fortune in gems and cannot turn around in a narrow passage: “Suit, you bless me, and you curse me.”
  • Janaila goes everywhere the ship cannot, Borraidus sees everything she cannot; neither gets anywhere alone, and they spend the game keeping score of each other’s offenses.
  • Aiah keeps everyone around her alive and can bear no weight; she rises only while she is being struck.

The consequence for the writing is the interesting part. A character built this way needs few lines to exist: the personality is already in how the hero moves, and dialogue only makes it legible. Blow says as much: the mechanical version of a character “kind of has a personality, but it’s subtle,” and story makes it “more understandable and front-facing” (interview with Arc Games). It is also why the heroes can fall silent late in the game without vanishing: the player keeps playing their characters long after they stop talking inference

Self-sabotage and the “mind behind the mind”

Isidore suspects that behind our clumsiness stands a hidden mind that drops the tray of reagents or puts an unfortunate “donkey” in our mouths to steer our lives where it wants them. Perhaps even the accident that made him who he is was “no accident at all. May be I wrecked my life intentionally, a mind behind my mind” (heroes2_wizard_and_priestess_alone_third_end). The script itself reaches for more down-to-earth examples: a note on the tray scene reads “Todo: Some more explanation here; want to make a mess why? To avoid an appointment, to not take the medicine I know I’m supposed to take?” (heroes2_wizard_and_priestess_alone_first). The Guild’s ships stop themselves on seeing a sign. The Trader once fled the mirrors, and his clone still calls that fear wisdom. In all three cases a body follows someone else’s script, “built into the bones.” In a game where the Visitor literally steers the heroes, the “mind behind the mind” is the player inference

“Softly and suddenly vanished away”

Before each of his Warehouses, a note says, the Founder Lemur read “a poem, half of nonsense, about a monster that makes people disappear”: Lewis Carroll’s The Hunting of the Snark (1876; audiolog_sky_invocation). The monster is the Boojum: whoever finds the Snark, if the Snark turns out to be a Boojum, vanishes. This is how the poem ends:

In the midst of the word he was trying to say,
In the midst of his laughter and glee,
He had softly and suddenly vanished away—
For the Snark was a Boojum, you see.

Lewis Carroll, The Hunting of the Snark, Fit the Eighth

Carroll’s contemporaries were already asking what the poem meant. In 1897 he answered: “To the best of my recollection, I had no other meaning in my mind, when I wrote it: but people have since tried to find the meanings in it,” and the one he liked best was that it “may be taken as an Allegory for the Pursuit of Happiness.” It is the Director’s dilemma: noise, or a message (chapter IX) inference The critic Matthewmatosis, recalling this recording, named “Jabberwocky”; the note points to the Snark, but his idea of nonsense that may hide meaning fits the Snark even better (chapter XII).

The game opens with just such a vanishing: the Queen breaks off her prayer (“But I—”), a pop, and she is gone. Almost everyone the Order invites vanishes from their world too: the Trader is invited to “depart this world,” Reynard leaves by a “special transport” his son was not to follow, the Caver “will not return directly home, not yet,” and the doorstead closes behind Leamington.

The game even keeps a Boojum in its code code A beam that runs off the edge of the world ends “at infinity,” and whoever steps along it simply leaves: no death animation, no body; the doors of the level break and the sound goes muffled (chapter III). A goblin taken this way, a note says, disappears “into the infinite,” and Tif’s “Just ... gone ...” is the poem’s last line in two words inference The poem left one more trace, in the South: the Bellman’s “perfect and absolute blank” of a sea chart turns up as Janaila’s first treasure (chapter IV).

hypothesisThe Order as the Boojum

Seen from those worlds, the Order is the Boojum: meet it, and you softly and suddenly vanish away. The Founder’s ritual reading would then be the Order’s joking self-portrait, recited at the entrance of every Warehouse he built.

The Acting Director picked up “lavish expenditure” from Reynard, and her “See you later, Alligator” turns up in the mouths of both her recruits: Reynard flings it at the heroes, and Leamington signs off with it, “As my mentor is fond of saying” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_5). In this game a borrowed phrase is a fingerprint of who has spent time with whom inference Applied to the Surveyor, the same test gives a startling result (chapter IV).

And in every world, beside the one being led blindfolded, there is an insider who knows more than they let on: in the North, Reynard himself; in the West, the Dragon; in the East, the Mirrorwright; in the South, the Surveyor and Vedessud, and by the end Borraidus too. Whether the South had a deal of its own, and with whom, the game doesn’t say inference

Multicolored light

The woman who appeared to Reynard was “bathed in multicolored light.” The heroes of the North face a “color fringe” with a vast land beyond it. The Trader’s special mirror is set “within a perimeter of polychromic light.” “The iridescence admits” the Surveyor, as it did Janaila. Even the dragon’s egg is “iridescent.” The rainbow boundary seems to be the visible seam where the Order’s space touches a world inference At the first meetings between worlds, heroes are puzzled by “weird red ... energies,” perhaps the same seam between two worlds. In the West, beams never appear as white light at all: they come already split into colors, each a separate law of motion, and the prism is the tool that steps from one law to the next. The rainbow at the seams may be the same split seen from outside: one light, broken into the rules of many worlds hypothesis

Paperwork in paradise

From the movers to the demiurges, everyone in this game is tired of work, and the game makes no secret of it inference Reynard’s crisis is a crisis of “paperwork, maintenance.” Isidore, surrounded by foul creatures, wishes he had started “some company about being sedentary”: guards for “buildings where nobody goes,” “no walking, we just sit there. Warming our feet by the fire,” and the script’s note sighs that this is simply how private guards work in the USA (heroes2_postmeetup_wizard). The Trader’s clone is sick of “Hassle, hassle” and the week’s merchantman. The Caver, at the very end: “I spent so long fixing up this suit... then, so long in the suit... It’ll be nice to be done for a bit” (end3_suit). And the Order, whose members live for centuries and build worlds, talks like a studio in crunch: ten years on one Warehouse, worlds swapped out after years of work (“Poof”), beautiful puzzles shelved in Cold Storage, designers consoled that “the real product here is the refinement of your soul,” colleagues gossiping that an invocation sounded “pretentious and hollow,” and a “kerfuffle” with the Preservation Crew that the Director settles with “what they don’t know won’t hurt them.” Heaven, here, is a workshop with office politics. It is the most disarming thing about the game’s grand claims, and probably the most autobiographical.

Everything is connected

Blow has said that Thomas Pynchon’s Gravity’s Rainbow carried him through the exhausted middle of The Witness and set the bar for his next game. When he started reading it, he thought, “This is impossible. How will I finish?”; what he took away was that “The book was saying ‘This kind of thing you want to do is possible’” (Killscreen, 2014). There is no Pynchon in the files. But two of the novel’s load-bearing ideas run through this game, and seeing them sharpens both inference

  • Paranoia and anti-paranoia. Pynchon calls paranoia the discovery that everything is connected, and anti-paranoia the state in which nothing is, “a condition not many of us can bear for long.” The characters drift into the first: Isidore suspects “some mind behind the mind” that stages his clumsiness, Tif feels watched, Leamington begins to see “Silken strands” between the worlds. In the Warehouse they are simply right: the watchers exist. Then the game turns the screw. The Order has built a cosmos where everything connects to everything, and that is exactly what rots it: the decay sets in where “the cross-correlation has become too dense.” The cure is engineered anti-paranoia: firewalls between the brigades, isolation, “a low-density restart.” Life is possible only in the narrow band between no connections and too many, just as surprise lives between the too predictable and the too random (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_3).
  • The elect and the preterite. Pynchon’s theology divides the world into the elect, chosen from above, and the preterite, passed over, and his sympathy lies with the passed-over. The game has its elect: the Trader, “as only you have been invited”; Reynard, who “at least had to be judged worthy of an invite.” But the Warehouse ends up in the hands of the two the Order never called. “We invited neither of you,” the Acting Director tells Leamington, of him and the Queen (end1_dimension_1); one becomes its keeper, the other earns its highest rank.

Water, judgement and treasure

  • Water is different in every world: dense and deadly for the Sailor and the Trader, breaking into drops for the heroes and the Caver, “eaten” of all energy in the Deadwaters. The meetings between worlds keep tripping over it, and that is how the game shows that worlds obey different laws.
  • Judgement. Reynard prides himself on his Judgement and is a judge by profession. Leamington feels “judged by a watchful eye” and asks: “Is this a test? Do I earn distinction by passing?” (audiolog_g_work_and_care). The endings are full of judges’ verdicts, Reynard “had to be judged worthy of an invite,” and Janaila calls him “Judgy.” Milton’s “Providence” is judgement too, only a just one.
  • Treasure. Every hero has their own glitter: Tif reaches for everything shiny, the Caver hoards gems and finds excuses, Janaila risks her life for six chests, the Trader built an empire. The game teases this kindly: everyone’s real treasure is the understanding they carry away.

Easter eggs and small things

The things that are easy to miss: a dedication, the team’s inside jokes, director’s notes, traces of cut content and cultural references.

In memory of Zach Polansky

The credits contain a separate line: “In memory of Zach Polansky” (credits_zach). Zach, known in the puzzle world as Jack Lance, worked at Thekla and died in 2023 at the age of 25; Blow called him one of the best game designers in the world. His traces show up in four places in the game:

  • The stand with the history of the map shows “Zach’s ground floor draft” (sign_overworld_design_2), and the next stand is already the closed, “wrapping” floor.
  • In the revisions hall, the commentator says of his level: “We don’t know why Zach made this a Thief level” (audiolog_di_across_the_fence_old). And there is no longer any way to ask him.
  • His name is in the internal names of levels: four sidestone_zach_* in the Mirrors and Stones zone, zach_lilies_v3 at Crossroads Low and push_and_pull_zach among Heroes and Water, while a variant of one of them, sidestone_zach_2_end2, stands in the hall of the second ending (per the fan map and the code).
  • His own games read like sketches of this game’s ideas inference In Enigmash the world is divided into regions with their own rules, and an object moving from one to another starts obeying the new ones: colliding worlds in miniature. In his July 2026 talk in China, Blow singled it out as an idea nobody has yet developed into a big game. Vext Edit puts the player in the puzzle designer’s chair, like the third ending. Another of his games was called Torus Tours.

Thekla, the Damascene School and the blueprint in the stars

Blow’s studio is named after the city of Thekla from Italo Calvino’s Invisible Cities. Its inhabitants build their city endlessly, because if the construction stopped, destruction would begin; and when asked for the blueprint, they wait for nightfall and point to the starry sky: there it is. For a game about an Order that builds Warehouses in a race against decay and calls the entrances to worlds “stars,” it would be hard to find a more fitting epigraph inference

Signs in the Lobby speak of the “Damascene School,” “active c. 2005-2030 AD”: the Order’s Founder “found inspiration in their methods” and adopted its logo and decor for “Lobby Theme 1a” (sign_original_star, sign_lobby_2). Those years coincide with the era of Braid, The Witness and this game. Perhaps the “Damascene School” is an in-game mask for Thekla itself, or for a circle of independent puzzle designers (the PuzzleScript community, say, which produced three of the source worlds), and the Order is their distant heir hypothesis Why “Damascene,” the game doesn’t explain; the candidates include damascening (inlaying steel with gold) and the apostle Paul’s revelation on the road to Damascus.

A number at the entrance and the leaves of Zork

The critic Matthewmatosis (chapter XII) noticed a number the game shows at the very start: 69,104. It isn’t in the game’s text files, presumably because it is part of an image rather than a string to translate, so it can’t be checked against them. If it’s right, it is one less than one of the most famous numbers in game history. In the text adventure Zork, the command “count leaves” gets the reply “There are 69,105 leaves here”: a joke about a hero who spends hours counting a pile of leaves, and about a world that anticipated even that.

The number 69,105 itself, though, is in the game’s data code: it tags two gates right by the Crossroads, on the row of the ruby lift by which the Visitor first rises to the upper tier (chapter VIII). Such a number merely links gates to their buttons and could have been anything. And the lobby’s code has a sign with the telling name intro_69_sign, whose text at rank zero differs from what it shows later. Perhaps it is the one that shows the number at the entrance, and it changes after the first ending hypothesis

hypothesisThe “arcane and auspicious” number

The Acting Director says that by the Order’s 250th anniversary “the serial numbers of the Warehouses were about to reach an arcane and auspicious number,” an inside joke “that few understood except Lemur, Director and some other Highests,” and that on the very day ground was broken for this Warehouse, the next facility was announced as “something new and big” (audiolog_sky_combiners_2). The Director speaks of the facilities built “across these dozen decades, all seventy thousand of them” (audiolog_u_decay_1). If 69,104 is this Warehouse’s number, the next one, the Tetratetrad, would be number 69,105: the leaves of Zork. Arcane to anyone who never played the text adventures of the late 1970s, auspicious to the generation that grew up on them, Blow’s generation. And the gates with that number stand in a meaningful spot: beyond them begins the way up, toward the next and bigger thing. The game doesn’t confirm it.

Internal names and menus

  • The game is written in Jai, Blow’s own programming language. The type descriptions Jai leaves in the executable are what let xPaw reconstruct the game’s internal structures, the source of many details in this chapter.
  • The input action modestly labeled “Niche Action” in the settings is called FriendlyDragon internally. Behind it is a real pet: a single friendly dragon lives on the Overworld code It breathes no fire; press this action next to it and it will follow the Queen, and if she walks into it, they swap places. In the saves examined, the counter of these toggles never rose above zero, and the game never reads it anyway (chapter VIII).
  • A comment in the file: “Patrick says we’re probably going to shuffle the inputs around.” Next to it, a complaint: “I feel like Forward and Backward are kind of ambiguous in this kind of game,” which is why they are labeled “Up” and “Down.”
  • The menu has an item called “Good-Looking Levels” and a Cheats section: “Unlock This Level,” “Solve This Level,” “Toggle Walking Through Walls.” There is a “Very Performance” graphics mode and a “Custom Screenshot” resolution marked “Only used by developers.”
  • Character voices can be switched from English to Chinese (menu_voice_language).
  • Save names are drawn from each world’s vocabulary: for the North that’s Warrior, Thief, Wizard, Bard, Druid, Goblin, Dragon, Crystal, Gate. The Priestess isn’t on the list: she keeps silent even here.
  • The demo’s strings promised “100% free-range human voice acting” and subtitles in 11 languages, and Demo Phase 2 unlocked on June 19, 2026.
  • The scene in which Reynard lets Valens out of his cage (heroes_mouse) hangs on the event cage_disappear, and its level has a special type of its own in the code, HEROES_CAGE_DISAPPEAR. There are only four such special types code: the Overworld, the lobby, the first ending and this cage.
  • The characters’ internal names don’t always match the credits code: Janaila is castaway, the Priestess cleric, the Caver moonman (in the subtitles, gemsman), and the dragons’ sound events are named gargoyle_*. Among the icons of the move history sits CHOP_TREE, “chop a tree,” though in the finished game trees are never felled: the Druid turns them into crystals inference
  • The internal names of levels are a layer of humor of their own code: to_kill_a_goblin, how_to_train_your_dragon, who_you_gonna_call, the_line_must_be_drawn_HERE (Picard in Star Trek); games: missile_command, yars_revenge_setup_v3, ice_climbers, snipperclips_square, dragonsweeper (the name of a game by Daniel Benmergui, who is himself credited under “Design from Community and Friends”); the theory of computation: busy_beaver, hanoi_3; office life: team_building_exercise, trust_falls. And sunk_cost_2_v3 is a sunk cost in a world of sinking stones, the same pun that names xPaw’s project. There is more for the patient: classic puzzles, wolf_boat_carry (the wolf, the goat and the cabbage crossing a river), prisoners_dilemma_alt, numberlink_alt; scripture and economics, little_faith_alt, breaking_bread, four_horsemen, invisible_hand; pop culture, beam_me_up_v3, mission_impossible, purple_haze, and assassins_creed in Heroes and Depth, a zone of falls (the leap of faith); old games again, defender and an Ikaruga tucked into infinite_looping_skipper_mirrored_ikaruga_alt; and the movers’ old trade, loading_docks_v2.

Signs

  • It is an Order habit to capitalize the names of gameplay objects: Sign, Crystal, Mirror, Beamcaster. A note to Leamington’s signs says so outright (end2_sokoban_writing_choice_1).
  • At the entrance: “Occupants: 0. You may proceed through the introduction” (sign_start_tutorial) and “Next Available Entry Window: (now).”
  • The sokoban tutorial: “Two Rocks are too many (for you).” The parenthetical is a joke for those who already know the Warrior.
  • “WARNING: Difficult and Unrewarding.”
  • “Gate will Remain Active whilst monsters are present.” Road signs: “Goblin Fortress 2 miles ------>,” “1 mile,” “1/2 mile,” “This Way ------>.”
  • “THIS WAREHOUSE IS A 4-COMBINATOR in D(3,1,1)+MCu111. Take appropriate measures.” The designation is never explained. Here is one way to read it. D(3,1,1) looks like a dimensional signature: three spatial dimensions, one of time, and one more, perhaps the “bulk” into which the Order has been “seeding” its spaces since 2271, or a second, “orthogonal” axis of time since 2276. In surface physics, Cu(111) is a face of a copper crystal, a classic substrate on which atomically thin layers are grown. Unless it is simply a joke in the style of hazard classes, “+MCu111” may point to the “substrate” Leamington wondered about (audiolog_g_substrate) hypothesis
  • The quotation from “Ulysses” on the sign is slightly off: the game has “the utmost bounds of human thought,” while Tennyson has “bound.”
  • The captions in the revisions hall form a vocabulary of polishing: Initial, Elaborated, Expanded, Reduced, Tuned, Simplified, Simplified again, Cleaned up, Compacted, Economized, Economized again, Streamlined, Clarified, Reimagined, Focused (sign_design_*). For each world there is a trio of “original game, mid-development art, final art.” And the Promesst stand mentions “behavior changes of Strength Beam, Cart, and Displacer” (sign_design_promesst_2).

Director’s notes

The lines in the subtitles come with the director’s notes (lines starting with ++): how to play a phrase, what a character knows and doesn’t know. According to the credits, the script was “Written by Jonathan Blow” (credits_voice_general). Some notes say more about the world and the heroes than the lines themselves:

  • On Isidore’s dream of guards who just sit by the fire, a working note (a Todo): “Maybe this whole sequence is really not cute at all because this is how all private guards are in the USA anyway, so it’s just, like, documenting reality” (heroes2_postmeetup_wizard).
  • On the Surveyor’s lines on the cliffs, also in Todo notes: “This is Warrior’s line; she should not say it” (about “Oh my God!”) and “She also probably shouldn’t say ‘Simple.’ Druid said that” (3d_intro_staircase, 3d_intro_carry). Every hero has a vocabulary, and the notes guard it, though both lines are still in the files.
  • On Reynard’s “proper time”: it’s proper time as in Einsteinian relativity, but “more pronounced, because when you’re in a different space that is disconnected, your rate of passage of time is not necessarily correlated” (audiolog_s_magistrate).
  • On the English of the future: “inaswell,” per the note, is “a word that came into use in the future; think of it like ‘insofar’” (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_1); the Director says “even solitarier” (audiolog_u_decay_4); and Leamington, after a couple of years with the Order, “has caught some of the habits of modern speech, but not all of them” (end1_dimension_0).

Unscrubbed files

The texts shipped not quite scrubbed, and that is a pleasure of its own: through the notes you can peer into the workshop: what was slated for a rewrite, how the text was fitted to the recordings, and what never got finished.

  • Cuts still in the text. The Director’s account of the Tetratetrad carries the notes “Todo: Cut ‘become frustrated’” and “Todo: Cut this quote,” yet both are still in the file, as is the “Double occurrence of the word ‘work’” that was also due for a fix (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1; chapter VII). The explanation of interdicts comes with “Todo: Rewrite: ‘have embedded scriers’ is awkward” (ssnew_built_in_2).
  • A lost line. The Trader’s “That fellow?” in the coda never made it into the subtitles (chapter V).
  • An anthem never recorded. The northern storyline’s last exchange, “a paean or a dirge?”, carries the note “Todo: meant to change paean to anthem; did this not get recorded?” (heroes3_victory_lap_end).
  • Text fitted to the voice. Notes signed “Charles” (apparently Charles Taylor, one of the programmers in the credits) report: “This used to read ‘Multiply the number by itself,’ I changed to match audio” (audiolog_u_computation); “Audio skips the ‘And’” (audiolog_u_wizard_mirror_worlds_connect). And of the ship’s last cry he writes: “‘Seaward heyyyaooo’ could be overlapped with a -1.59 relative time. Seemed too rogue for me to just do that though...” (skipping_stones_done_treasures_0).
  • Slips that stayed. The psalm recording says “were none of them” instead of the biblical “was none of them” (psalm_139_part_2); the ceremony line “Through the years, as you continue” carries “Todo: Revise this line” (end1_dimension_4); and the Director’s “It’s so funny” gets a doubtful “Todo: funny?” (audiolog_u_decay_4).
  • Typos. In the lines: Tif’s “I supppose,” Leamington’s “a beneficient God” and “each withother,” the Director’s “litle nuggets of gold,” the Acting Director’s “We would constuct,” the Surveyor’s “phase” for faze, and “chidren” in the Queen’s prayer, in all three versions of the scene. In the notes: “solumn,” “proivde,” “omany,” “prevoius.”
  • Notes cut off mid-sentence. “She’s been looking for” (on the Bard and the crystals), “As brought up in the following conversation as well,” (on the burned town), “‘And we get a bit of a rest’ is” (on the treasures).
  • A white lie. The fully assembled versions of the prologue and the coda are tagged with the Director’s voice from start to finish, and the note owns up: “Here we lie and say the speaker is ‘director’ the whole time,” so that the scene can play from a single file (pre_coda_scene_assembled, coda_scene_assembled). These duplicates were left out of the word count (chapter I).
  • Cut, but not erased. The code keeps traces of ideas that aren’t in the game code Among the artifacts, next to the rod, the torc and the prism, it describes a “speed control” in three grades and an “undo side panel,” yet no level holds them: apparently these interface features were once meant to be found inference Among the beam colors sits a deprecated EGG_HATCH, a “hatching” beam; in the release, the egg is opened by a story event. Also marked deprecated: one-time lines for the first gem “feeding,” the first torc and the first prism, and two flags, “entered the exit chamber without the dragon” and “with the dragon.” It looks as if the Promesst ending once branched: the Caver left the mines with or without the dragon ambassador hypothesis And the notes designers leave right inside levels have, besides tags like Design, Bug and Story, a tag called Meh; playtesters get notes of their own, with a name and a time.

Arguing with the game

The critic Matthewmatosis, himself the author of the puzzle game Logic Bombs, finished the game in 130 hours and recorded an “audio log” of his own, full of praise and objections. He suspects Blow placed the recordings around the Warehouse to cut off the easy avenues of discussion in advance. Checked against the files, the suspicion holds: most of his points are already raised somewhere in the game, sometimes in his own words. But raising an objection is not answering it, and below the two are kept apart: his point, what the game actually says, and who comes out ahead. Some rounds go to the game; several go to him. “Pretentious”: a word the game already knows

The critic

Right after The Witness he might have called it pretentious, and he now takes the word back: pretension means pretending, and Blow is plainly interested in what he talks about. The recording that plays beside Blow’s name in the credits, he thinks, took courage.

The game tells this story itself, and tells it first. The Acting Director recalls how, before she even made Implementor, she imitated the Founders and recorded a solemn “invocation” at the entrance of her first Warehouse. “What I had done felt true,” she says. And then:

But, people talked. Some said I sounded pretentious and hollow. And maybe I was, a little. But we all have to start somewhere.

the Acting Director, audiolog_sky_invocation

The members who came after the Founders didn’t do any of this: “it was just, basic instructions and help, and let’s go.” Perhaps they felt they didn’t rank highly enough “to put on such a show,” that it “would be presumptuous”; “I think also, they were worried about embarrassing themselves.” She kept it up out of stubbornness (“Can’t just stop and show the gossips that they were right!”), then fell out of the practice, “Or perhaps I’d let the gossips win after all.” And after her talks with Leamington she records the invocation again: “With it, I have reopened a part of myself I’d kept shut away for a very long time.” A note says this is the Paradise Lost prayer at the start of the game.

In the credits, a voice tagged jon reads the same text and adds: “Unto ages of ages. Thy will be done. Amen” (paradise_lost_invocation_credits). The game first tells what anyone risks by stepping out with a prayer, and only then does the author take that risk himself inference Blow names the risk in so many words: the game takes on “what are we doing here, what is life about” “in a way that most people would be a little bit too embarrassed to put in a script for a video game” (livestream with Edmund McMillen). The critic and the heroine use the same test: not “does it sound grand?” but “is it true?”

The critic adds that Blow may yet miss the days when people called him pretentious: now they may call him arrogant, for “one of the more audacious of the 4-combiners,” for the “shallows” of those who build smaller, for the ancestors’ “primitive games.” But each such line has a hedge beside it: “our Order holds this time in great respect” (audiolog_u_games_inspirations), “we’re powerful, but we aren’t magic” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_2), “This is deeply questionable, but we have to start somewhere” (audiolog_sky_10000_things), “It’s a little humbling to know that your puzzles could always be better” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2).

The critic, in a follow-up

The invocation log sits on the critical path, so everyone hears it. It also makes the Acting Director the clearest stand-in for Blow: he repeats her words himself, which he never does for Leamington or the Director-on-Sabbatical, and the critic takes that link to be deliberate. It places Blow below the Sabbatical Director, which supports the humility reading; so does the reverence paid to Lemur, a nod to the developers who built the art before him. But humility can be taken too far. If Blow sees himself as one of the most influential puzzle designers of his time, he should, because he is; only an authority is not guaranteed to be right about everything.

The game stages both halves of that balance inference Reverence for those who came first is built into the Order’s furniture: the Lobby is “an homage to the works of the Damascene School,” a theme that “later fell into disuse, but here we bring it back” (sign_lobby_1, sign_lobby_2), and the Founder is still consulted about the shape of the next Warehouse after 250 years. The game itself is built from four other designers’ free games, and their names stand in the credits as the authors of its source worlds (chapter I). Pride is treated as fuel and as danger at once. The Acting Director kept up her invocation out of pride (“Can’t just stop and show the gossips that they were right!”), and the Director’s invocation ends on Leviathan, “a king over all the children of pride” (chapter X). And the critic’s caveat about authority is the game’s own refrain: “Sometimes you’d be right. But you must always really think it through” (audiolog_u_wizard_mirror_worlds_connect); “It’s hard to be fully confident we found them all” (judge_wizard_jailbreak_correct). A confident maker who knows he can be wrong: that is the portrait the game draws, and the critic signs it.

“Why’s this place so big?”

The critic

Blow is a maximalist hiding behind classical restraint: each world in Braid has its own gimmick, The Witness has panels outside its unified system, and here there’s a combinatorial explosion. The more there is, the harder it is to trust that the game showed the most interesting interactions. And yet quantity is a quality of its own: “puzzles are the sincerest form of content,” because a puzzle can’t be copied and pasted like a pack of enemies.

In the game, Leamington asks this nearly word for word: “if you’re trying to achieve clarity, then, why’s this place so big? To be blunt, it was a disorienting place to land. Would have preferred something smaller!” (audiolog_sky_why_so_big). The Acting Director grants that there’s another way: some Implementors “still like to keep it simple and build places smaller, and more focused, than they could do.” But “if you stay in the shallows, you won’t learn to swim. Not as well. Not under harrowing circumstances.” Then she concedes the opposite of what Leamington asked: “we left some things out. It could have been bigger, ‘should’ have been bigger perhaps.” For the Order, maximalism is doctrine: Lemur rejects a 5-combiner as “Too small,” an 8-combiner with “why stop there?”, and the motto of the whole venture is “Teach them to yearn for the vast and endless sea” (chapter VII).

And the critic’s praise, that a successful author didn’t retire but put his earnings into a huge and risky puzzle game, is something the Order states as its own principle: “We didn’t want safe... we wanted to fashion our own small Leviathan, an endeavor at which we might truly suffer failure” (audiolog_sky_combiners_4).

The game shares the critic’s doubt as well. The commentator in the revisions hall admits that for a game of significant size “it’s very very hard to get anywhere close to the best versions of all the puzzles” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2); the hologram, that “It’s hard to be fully confident we found them all” (judge_wizard_jailbreak_correct). And the Order’s history names the root of the problem: the earliest Warehouses mixed individual objects, and “Beyond a certain size, the outcomes tended towards inscrutability.” That is why the Order began borrowing whole worlds (audiolog_sky_combiners).

The Director confirms the “sincerest form of content” line from the other side. Whenever the Tetratetrad’s overall shape changed, “the optimal form of any individual puzzle changes in response”; all ten thousand puzzles had to be adapted, and “Very beautiful puzzles were regularly dropped into Cold Storage,” because “the environment for which they were designed no longer existed” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_2). A puzzle can’t be copy-pasted, and it can’t always be moved: change the structure around it, and a beautiful design may stop fitting inference Blow adds the other side of the ledger. The combinations proved so fertile that the team had to force itself to stop, and could easily have made at least five hundred more good puzzles (talk in China, July 2026). That supports the critic’s doubt as much as it answers it: what the game shows is a selection, and nobody outside the studio can check whether it was the best one.

Four games or four windows

The critic

The “four games” framing is somewhat arbitrary. Skipping Stones and Mirror Isles were both designed by Alan Hazelden; had he merged them himself, we’d think of them as one game, and Blow would have needed a different fourth. He sees the four games more as “different windows looking into the same thing,” and admits to a fondness for Platonism about whether math is invented or discovered.

The game itself counts ideas, not games: “In the limit it’s 2 to the nth power, where n is the number of ideas across all the worlds” (audiolog_sky_combiners). And the worlds don’t contribute equally: the North and West “import a large number of objects and behaviors that serve as combination fuel,” while the East and South “only bring in a few objects and behaviors each, which is more typical” (audiolog_sky_why_so_big_3). For the Tetratetrad, the choice of worlds was openly revised: “we kept Tetrising the tetrads,” there was “a farm league of candidate tetrads,” and an Implementor might “work with a particular world for years, only to find it swapped out one day. Poof” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1). The Order agrees with the critic: the set of worlds isn’t sacred; it’s the current best answer to an optimization problem inference

And “windows looking into the same thing” is the Order’s metaphysics in its purest form. It sources its worlds from the “Ultimate Ensemble,” Tegmark’s set of all mathematical structures, and the Director’s Mandelbrot lesson runs: “the math is so simple, obviously we don’t put the complexity in there, ourselves. That complexity and structure is just already there somehow” (audiolog_u_computation). Roger Penrose used the same example to argue for mathematical Platonism in The Emperor’s New Mind (1989): “The Mandelbrot Set is not an invention of the human mind: it was a discovery,” and “Like Mount Everest, the Mandelbrot Set is just there!” Where the critic is half a Platonist, the Order is a whole one inference And the game adds a version of Penrose’s argument of its own: Leamington’s world, without computers, found the same set and called it the Clayson’s Field (chapter IX). Two worlds, two names, one shape.

Blow’s own answer to “arbitrary” is functional, not historical inference The grouping into four games is what keeps the combinatorial web from turning to mush in the player’s head (chapter I); the first plan had six, and the four that shipped were simply first on the list. On history, then, the critic is right: the line between Hazelden’s two games is an accident of how they happened to be released. On pedagogy, the four boxes earn their keep.

The critic’s own map of contributions is worth keeping: the Heroes bring many player characters, the beams many ways to interact with terrain, the mirrors are “this game’s portal,” awkward until they become intuitive, and the stones and lilies bring ongoing movement and a sense of timing.

Reading the level

The critic

The third ending names a habit he dislikes: “reading the level.” Once everything unnecessary is stripped away, conspicuous details give the answer away. In one late skipping-stone puzzle, a piece of terrain “sticks out a little bit too far compared to the rest of the island”; he put the mirror there, kicked the stone, and was 90% done without thinking about the goal. He prefers Zachtronics-style problems, where the goal is clear and the path is open. For him, reading the level is a bug; for Blow, a feature.

For Blow it really is a feature, and the game says so. The sign “Reading the Level” calls this skill the bridge “from player to designer”: entering a level, you asked yourself “what was the designer intending to make happen by arranging the walls in a specific way?” and “What is the geometry telling me that I can and cannot do?” (sign_end3_reading_the_level_*). The third ending’s course describes the player as a reader of terrain, and on a skipping-stone level at that: “Why is there so much unused territory on the main isle?”; “When trying to understand the objective, often your player will first look at the exit area to see what is necessary to win,” and unneeded terrain is bad because “the player will spend time perceiving and understanding” it (judge_simplify_skipping_2–4). The judge even asks the critic’s own question: “Does he even need the Rock? If not, why is it there?” (judge_mirror_4_islands_4). The designer asks “why is this here?” first and removes everything that has no answer; that is why the same question works so well in the player’s hands inference

But the game knows the price. In the revisions hall, readability is a virtue: a version with two Gates is bad because “it’s now very hard to read the level” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified), and another is “somewhat misleading because it places the Goblin into a suspicious position” that is “not part of the solution” (audiolog_di_sender). Yet of the final version of the same level, the commentator says the extra confusion could be removed, “but then maybe we are closer to just giving the player the answer, which doesn’t feel good to me. All of this is subjective,” and he leaves the Gate “slightly hidden,” “more like a stylistic flourish” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2). The textbook of the third ending asks you to restore a Rock that sat “on the wrong parity, intentionally”: it offers a second place from which to try teleporting to the exit, and that place is a trap (judge_simplify_wizard_5). Reading the level is fought by hand.

The course even praises the very thing the critic caught inference Approving one placement of a skipping stone, the judge says: “I like the way that the Stone lands in the neck of that western peninsula” (judge_end3_skipping_1_4). Terrain shaped to receive a stone is the lesson, not an accident. And the course teaches the opposite trick too. Of a Thief level with goblins, the judge says the point would be stronger if the goblins stood farther apart, “because the extra space would bait the player into thinking it might be possible to pull the Crystal through” (judge_make_thief_goblin_ok). The designer does not merely put up with the player reading the level. He baits it.

The critic’s favorite levels, the Beamcaster ones, show the other antidote: there one beam serves several purposes, so “why is this here?” stops giving hints. On this the game is split. One task calls the best solution the one where “Every Beam is used twice” (judge_end3_redundant_beams_1); the revisions hall warns that using objects for several purposes sometimes “just fogs up the core idea” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified).

Immersion and fresh eyes

The critic

He values a particular kind of immersion: playing without thinking of the game as a game. Blow, he feels, appreciates games as crafted objects, and the more you play, the harder it is to see them any other way; for the critic that is more a frustration than a gift. No wonder the Director-on-Sabbatical says the Order goes back to old games “to return to a childlike state.”

At first the game’s physics seems to side with the critic, but read the line closely. Fresh eyes are a resource, and the Director defines them narrowly: “those who have never before experienced a particular Warehouse, when it’s all new to them, somehow their activities strengthen the projections for a while” (audiolog_u_decay_3). In the same breath he tells Leamington, no innocent by then, that there are “a few more Warehouses I’d like you to visit.” What the Order needs is a first visit, not a naive visitor. That is why no one may speak to the Visitor, “We are not supposed to pollute you, you see” (end2_sokoban_writing_choice_1), and why Leamington writes so briefly in the second ending, “to give space for the Puzzles themselves to speak” (chapter VIII): the Order protects the first time.

So the third ending, which teaches the Queen to read levels and to design them, costs her nothing in the Order’s physics inference She can still be a fresh observer anywhere she has not been. The innocence the critic misses is something else: not seeing a game as a made thing at all. The game has no use for that innocence; its whole course is built to end it. Here the two simply want different things from a player.

Riven’s atmosphere, or a workshop

The critic

Blue Prince and Void Stranger, and Riven before them, envelop you in an atmosphere that goes beyond the puzzles: there is a world to get into and a story that can affect you in ways that are hard to define. Here he feels “in there” less.

The game knows that road and tells us how it left it. The Order’s first constructions, the Acting Director says, “were not too unlike these old entertainments, highly fictionalized, so they used a similar technique. Over time it blossomed into something more direct.” What they used to term “world-building” is “funny to think. We’d have to call it differently now” (audiolog_s_tradition_of_our_people_part_1). The Witness was still a mysterious island. The Warehouse is an open workshop: a sign at the entrance announces “THIS WAREHOUSE IS A 4-COMBINATOR” (sign_facility), and inside there is a revisions hall full of drafts. The workshop framing explains the choice; it does not show that the resulting atmosphere works, and on that the critic’s 130 hours are better evidence than any line in the files inference

The game does know the price. The Order goes back to old games “to return to a childlike state, to try and notice what we’ve lost in the modern day” (audiolog_u_games_inspirations), and to it a first look at a place is literally a resource (above). The longing for immersion isn’t the game’s blind spot; it is one of its themes.

From the level to the world

The critic

Reading the level is teleological thinking: “why is this here?” assumes everything has a purpose. He considers the game “healthy,” good exercise for the brain, but suspects that a thousand such puzzles train you to see purpose in the world too. Perhaps Blow arrived at his own views this way: in The Witness he still seems on the fence about a higher power; here he leans toward one.

The Order describes exactly this training, openly, as a spiritual practice: “We play one specific situation, after another, after another. We learn to generalize... We absorb, to our current capacity, the Way of Creation.” Then comes “a special meditation wherein we take the knowledge we’ve internalized and expand it outward, meeting the world with what we know. Seeing the world in a new way.” At first seated, eyes closed; “Eventually you can do them eyes open, just walking around. Even when speaking with someone” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_1b). The critic’s suspicion is the Order’s stated program inference And the Acting Director names the result plainly: in the moment of insight, “you become someone greater, because your mind is now enlarged” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2_surprise).

But the game draws the line between a level and a world itself inference In the Director’s lecture, as encipherment improves, “this conspicuous structure disappears, and the message becomes nearly impossible to crack. Because it looks exactly like randomness”; whether a page is noise or a message, “You can’t know” (audiolog_u_chaos_2). A Warehouse is built the other way round: its structure is conspicuous because its builders want to be read, and the course on Clarity exists to make it more so. Reading levels works because levels are made to be read. The world, by the Director’s own model, need not be, so the habit cannot carry over as evidence. At most it teaches that the absence of visible design proves nothing, and that its presence proves something only where someone meant to be understood.

Inside the Warehouse, the argument from design is simply true: the builders exist. Leamington infers them from the gilded decks and cages and runs through the critic’s own options: “Is this a test? Do I earn distinction by passing? Is it a kind of training, to make me more capable of solving problems? Is it merely a show of grand workmanship, like the monuments of forgotten civilizations?” (audiolog_g_work_and_care). Then he meets the builders in person.

A ladder of gods

The critic

He senses a hierarchy of godlike entities: the Magistrate, above him the Acting Director, above her the Director on sabbatical, and the highest one we know of is away, busy with other things. Perhaps a metaphor for what the highest God is up to right now. Blow, he thinks, cast himself as the Acting Director rather than the one on sabbatical, below a higher power, and there’s some humility in that.

The credits do call him “The Director-on-Sabbatical” (credits_voice_overworld). Sabbatical goes back to the Sabbath, the day the Creator rested from all his work, and to the sabbatical year in which the land lay fallow. But this Director isn’t resting: “I’m still out there ... investigating. You’ll have to keep running things for now. Or appoint a successor if you’re tired.” Asked whether it’s going well: “It’s going somewhere. Well or poorly, too soon to say” (audiolog_u_meeting). Above him stands a still more distant figure: Lemur “prefers to keep his spindly hands off of day-to-day matters” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1). And above them all, the God to whom they pray.

This ladder stages a famous objection to the argument from design. In David Hume’s Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779, Part V), Philo points out that even if the world was designed, nothing shows its maker to be one, perfect or still present: “A great number of men join in building a house or ship... why may not several deities combine in contriving and framing a world?” The world might be “only the first rude essay of some infant deity,” “the work only of some dependent, inferior deity,” “the production of old age and dotage in some superannuated deity.” Hume meant it as mockery. In the Warehouse, all of it is literally true: it was built by a team, by someone only “Acting,” as “the very limit of what I could do,” possibly for the last time, and it decays. The Order are Hume’s small gods made flesh: living proof of his objection, and believers in a great God all the same inference

As for Blow, the files spread him across two rungs: his own voice (tag jon) reads the Acting Director’s prayer, while in the subtitles the developer commentary in the revisions hall carries the same tag, director, as the Director-on-Sabbatical. That tag is technical (the level data even gives the developer an author of his own, DEVELOPER), but the voice is a choice, and in a follow-up the critic reads it as the deliberate one: of the three voices, Blow repeats only hers (above). On his own ladder, the author stands a rung below the top inference

One scene the critic read the way the guardsman Jackays does. It seemed to him that the arrow itself phased the Queen out, perhaps shot by the Magistrate. A note in the prologue puts exactly this thought in Jackays’s head: “Did that just-shot arrow somehow make her magically disappear?” But the stage direction separates the causes: a pop as the Queen disappears, and “the arrow thuds into the tree right behind where she was kneeling”; the shooter is the “traitor” Jackays orders seized (coda). The arrow didn’t take her; it missed, because she was already gone. Still, the critic’s sense that the Order’s ladder reaches into her world agrees with the hypothesis in chapter VIII: the pop may have been the first step of Reynard’s plan.

Three voices, three seekers

The critic

The audio logs climb three levels. Leamington stands in for the player, with practical questions: how do I get through, how does this work, what is it made of. The Acting Director is game design, at times trite (in his retelling, “we’ll leave the recording in a tantalizing place”). The Director is the hope that there’s something more to games. The structure reminds him of Tarkovsky’s Stalker: the Professor, the Writer and the Stalker.

The match holds up better than one might expect. Tarkovsky’s Professor is a physicist, and the Director says nearly the same of Leamington: “You’re an engineer” (audiolog_u_chaos_1). The Writer is an artist afraid of losing his inspiration; the Acting Director is the maker of Warehouses whose invocation, some said, sounded “pretentious and hollow,” who “fell out of the practice” and built her Warehouse at “the very limit of what I could do.” The Stalker is the believing guide; the Director says of the Order’s work, “it’s all to the glory of God” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1). The Warehouse has something of the Zone, too: a territory where the usual laws don’t hold and you advance only by testing the path inference Blow has quoted Tarkovsky before: The Witness embeds a ten-minute sequence from Nostalghia (1983). Whether Stalker is a conscious model, the files don’t say.

There is a spatial side as well (chapter I): Leamington’s beacons play on the ground, the Acting Director’s talks up high, the Director’s down below, next to “Beam Computation.” The deeper the question, the farther from the ground, and the “deep research” the Director is “performing ... in the background” (audiolog_u_decay_1) is literally underground inference

Even the line he finds trite is about placement, and in the game it is drier than in his retelling: the second part of a talk goes “in a location that must be deliberately earned, but that is plainly visible on first entry,” so that the Visitor has “a goal to work toward ... if he’s interested” (audiolog_s_tradition_of_our_people_part_2; chapter IX).

The missing top floor

The critic

His most serious attack is on the structure of the endings. If the voices climb from practice to design to meaning, then the third ending, the design course, should have been the second, and the third should have been something on the Director-on-Sabbatical’s level. What, he doesn’t know, and he suspects Blow doesn’t either: Blow has said he makes games about questions he doesn’t know the answers to.

The game agrees that the top floor is unfinished, and says so. The Director’s investigation is “going somewhere. Well or poorly, too soon to say” (audiolog_u_meeting). The Tetratetrad is “the brightest star in our crown. Or it would be, when finished” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_3). And Leamington’s last recording ends on a threshold: he hopes to design a Warehouse of his own someday, “but before that can happen, there’s a problem they must solve that lies beyond my present learning”; erosion comes slowly, then as a landslide, “But if decay can come quickly, perhaps it can go quickly” (end3_sokoban_writing_choice_5).

The three endings are watching, intervening and building. The fourth step, understanding why built worlds die and how to save them, is left to a Director who is still “out there” inference The critic is right that it isn’t in the game. The game stops where its thinking stops, and that is honest: better no top floor than a painted one. Blow concedes the difficulty: of all his games’ philosophical backgrounds, this one is “maybe the most complicated one that’s hardest to explain” (interview with Arc Games). But honesty is not a payoff. The voices climb in an order that promises a summit, the game never builds it, and on structure this round goes to the critic inference

Belief: survival tactic or truth

The critic

The claim that the Order requires no specific beliefs struck him as naive: every society, even a game studio, shares something, if only “I don’t kill my co-workers.” The Acting Director, he thinks, appeals to objective morality and so to a godlike guarantor, when shared beliefs that no higher entity enforces would be enough.

Both sides cut a corner here. The critic’s first: the Acting Director never says the Order shares nothing. Asked whether it is a religious organization, she says members needn’t “believe anything specific,” and adds at once that those who don’t “understand that the structure and machinery of the Order were created according to these beliefs. ‘It’s in the water’” (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_3). “I don’t kill my co-workers” is exactly what she means by water: a shared rule built into the institution, not demanded as a creed.

Hers is in the evidence. “Because even if you make a boat out of rules, if everyone knows the rules are arbitrary, why not break them, if it feels good in the moment? How would you get everyone to observe arbitrary, meaningless, unbacked rules for even a single century?” And: “Many societies chose to be less-structured and to rely on the good judgement of their members, but if you want to know how well they worked, good luck finding anyone to ask.” That is a survivor’s argument, and survivorship cuts both ways: the dead societies can’t testify against her, but the Order’s survival doesn’t show that belief, rather than ambition (“Ambition helps too,” she admits) or luck, did the work. Her own society, which tried to live by a reconstruction of England before 1913, dissolved; her theory is also her biography. Her case is pragmatic, “Beliefs can keep you anchored. They have survival value” (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_2; a note calls this “a Darwinian framework of thought”), and pragmatism is all it proves.

And Leamington catches the gap between the two leaders. He tells the Director: “Your Acting said this is a survival tactic, and I understand that idea to a decent degree. But it seems you really believe it” (audiolog_u_chaos_1). The game contains both the critic’s functional view and the realism he rejects, and lets them disagree inference The first view carries a tension of its own: a belief held for its usefulness becomes fragile as soon as everyone knows why it is held, the very weakness the Acting Director sees in arbitrary rules inference

Evil, the cipher and the builder’s duty

The critic

Leamington raises the problem of evil, and the Director’s answer about compression and ciphers is a sidestep: even if the world is a compressed message, that doesn’t explain why conscious creatures must suffer so. And he adds, with a smile, that he might be a bit more optimistic himself if he had $20 million to spend on Sokoban; perhaps their different experiences shape their views.

The Director doesn’t promise more. Of his three models, enciphered, compressed or random, he says “You can’t know,” and offers the idea “even if only as a guiding image” (audiolog_u_chaos_2). In the philosophy of religion this move is close to skeptical theism (Stephen Wykstra, 1984): our failing to see a reason for suffering doesn’t show there is none. The standard objection to it is precisely the critic’s: it explains no actual suffering.

The game knows this tradition of the “sidestep” from the inside: the Director’s own invocation is Job 41, where God answers Job’s suffering with Leviathan, not with reasons (chapter X). And the game puts its practical answer not into theodicy but into the ethics of whoever builds worlds: “We hold a high position. We must not use it for cruelty” (judge_end3_mirror_1_4); “Please do not mess with the player! She needs a fair shot” (judge_fix_exploit1_0). Milton’s “justify the ways of God to men” becomes a demand on the builder inference

The $20 million line is about biography, not argument, and no argument answers it. The game’s own history merely turns it around: there, wealth bred nihilists, not optimists; people became “incredibly rich,” and “almost every way we employ these riches becomes deleterious to our well-being” (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_1). And what the critic thanks Blow for, a successful author who didn’t ride off into the sunset but put the money back into games, is what the game itself calls “lavish expenditure” (chapter X) inference

Whose surprise

The critic

The talk of a universe driving toward surprise strikes him as anthropocentric: a superintelligence, say a Dyson sphere, won’t be surprised by what surprises us, just as we aren’t surprised by what surprises a dog. Surprise is a joy of the game, but he isn’t sure it means as much as looking through the “Platonic window.”

In the game, surprise depends on the observer by definition: the composition “needs to be on the edge of the Visitor’s understanding” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2_surprise), and in information theory, too, the surprisal of an event is measured against the observer’s expectations. The Order already lives in the critic’s scenario. Its members are “artificially strengthened”; the Tetratetrad is “too dense, too hard to follow” for a newcomer, “but if they’d done a good ten or twenty other Warehouses first, they could work their way up to it” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_3); and the Acting Director warns Leamington that “Everything gets old at some point.” To surprise ever stronger minds, the Order raises density. And density is exactly what speeds the decay, whose limits, the Director says, may have to do with the mind: “Some think the bounds involve consciousness in some way” (audiolog_u_decay_3).

hypothesisThe decay as the price of surprise

Every step up in minds calls for a denser Warehouse, and density brings the decay closer. Then the “sinking star” is the fate of any civilization that lives by surprise. And the remedy the Order found is telling: “fresh observers help with preservation,” anyone seeing a given Warehouse for the first time (audiolog_u_decay_3). Not the naive player the critic longs for, but a new one: a civilization that lives by surprise ends up living off newcomers (above).

Play is older than games

The critic

The Director tells a history of games, not of play, and play predates games. The idea isn’t the whole value of a puzzle, or why would people do the newspaper sudoku every day or enjoy cosmetics? There’s pleasure in mimesis, in things in a game looking like things. His example is the Clarity demonstration: in the first version you push a robot past a dragon; in the final one, it’s just the robot and some walls. Clearer, but the dragon was fun.

The Director’s history really is a history of enforcing rules: “cards, or little wooden men,” then gears that “perform the logic,” so “The players no longer have to enforce the rules themselves” (audiolog_u_old_games). Johan Huizinga’s Homo Ludens (1938) begins the other way round: “Play is older than culture.” Yet the Order’s root concept is play: līlā, “a constant, effortless action, performed out of the sheer fullness of being” (chapter IX). In Roger Caillois’s terms (Man, Play and Games, 1958), the Order’s cosmos is busy with paidia, “the spontaneous manifestations of the play instinct,” and the Order turns it into ludus, “the taste for gratuitous difficulty”; Caillois counts crossword puzzles and “mathematical recreations” among the purest forms of ludus inference The brigade that built this Warehouse is the “Static Līlā Brigade”; the Dynamic Brigade is firewalled off from it (audiolog_u_decay_4), and the game never shows what it makes.

hypothesisThe other half of play

If “static” means a still puzzle with an intended solution, “dynamic” may be the half of play the critic misses: systems whose depth you discover for yourself, as in action games or at Zachtronics. The game doesn’t say so; this is only a reading of the name.

The critic, in a follow-up

He had overlooked the Dynamic Brigade, mentioned so rarely, and allows that there may be more play to be found in its works. All three of Blow’s games, he notes, would fall into the static category: they use randomness and dynamic elements only for cosmetic touches like particle effects. Blow’s next game, as he understands it, won’t be a puzzle game, so perhaps it will belong to the Dynamic Brigade; whatever it is, he looks forward to it.

The game leaves exactly that door ajar in its own vocabulary inference The Static Līlā Brigade built this Warehouse; its Dynamic counterpart stands behind a firewall, and the files never show what it makes. Braid, The Witness and this game are all still puzzles with intended solutions. Static cannot mean motionless here: stones skip in real time, and the critic himself credits them with “a timing element.” Blow’s rule is that a puzzle with an action element is built on the logic of timing, not on reflexes, and that any such level must be passable at a walk, without the run button (talk in China, July 2026). What makes a puzzle static is that its solution was designed, not that nothing moves. The other half of play is named in the Warehouse and left for later.

The revisions hall doesn’t reject the pleasure of silliness: “an unexpected and silly interaction of Goblins wearing Lilies as hats” (audiolog_di_lily_wearing_intro); “This design is me having fun with the Thief’s movement patterns” (audiolog_di_heroes_1_7_v2); dragons replace goblins because they make it “quicker and more comfortable to experiment” (audiolog_di_tiger_lily_v2). But fun stays only if it serves the topic: the lily-hat level loses its stone-pushing as “extraneous.” The ÆSTHETICS sign grants that “Your creations are more enjoyable, and land better in your player’s mind, when they look nicer,” yet makes visuals serve reading: “Colors and shapes determine what the player notices first” (sign_end3_visuals_1, sign_end3_visuals_5). Even the detail the critic praised, the ocean grid visible only in places, is a rule from the same course: the grid “should be visible in areas where it helps players count and align squares,” and not “in unhelpful areas where it can mislead or distract” (judge_environment_art_grid_wrong_ocean).

And the critic’s own example has a twist. To him, the Bard is mimesis laid over an abstract mechanic. But mimesis means imitation, and in the mechanics the Bard is imitation itself: everything within two tiles of her repeats her steps, and in the story she learns how each person “wants to be reached” (chapter II) inference

The fly is where the critic wins outright, and by Blow’s own rules inference The team that in Braid didn’t even give Tim an idle animation, so as not to distract anybody, left a fly in the Warehouse: in some biomes it buzzes from time to time, and it irritated the critic “almost every time.” Blow told his composers to avoid exactly this, since a sudden sound just before a breakthrough means “there goes your train of thought,” and he says the team doesn’t like “visuals that draw your eye to something that’s irrelevant to the gameplay” (Wookash Podcast; livestream with Edmund McMillen). The dragon in the Clarity demonstration is a different case: a living detail inside the puzzle, which the player can see, use or ignore at will, while the fly interrupts from outside it. One is mimesis the player controls, the other is noise the player suffers. The revisions hall’s “All of this is subjective” (audiolog_di_sender_simplified_v2) covers the dragon better than the fly.

A cage and two sticks

The critic

Stuck in a sparse level, he feels like an animal in a cage: dropped there by a higher power with two rocks and told to bang them together until it figures it out, “or you’re not getting any food.”

Psychology has a founding scene for exactly this. On Tenerife, between 1913 and 1920, Wolfgang Köhler gave chimpanzees bananas out of reach, plus crates and sticks. The apes stacked crates, and Sultan joined two sticks into a single tool. Köhler concluded that they solved by insight rather than trial and error: the same “BAM, with an insight, you see the answer” the Acting Director describes (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2_surprise). Sokoban is a game about pushing crates, and a sign in the tutorial jokes: “Two Rocks are too many (for you)” (sign_two_rocks).

The one difference: the Warehouse has no food. “I should expect to be in some alarm by now, for want of food and fresh water. But here I don’t seem to need nourishment,” Leamington marvels (audiolog_g_no_eating). The cage still pays out, though, in access: cages thaw, ranks rise, towers light, the map opens. The Warehouse is not free of extrinsic reward; it routes every reward through understanding, since the only way to open a cage is to have learned what it guards. Blow’s own image is not a banana but a level-up: solving puzzles is “the puzzle-knowledge equivalent of leveling up your character in an RPG,” a growing “snowball of knowledge” (Wookash Podcast). The critic’s complaint survives where that snowball stalls: in a sparse level, before the insight, a cage is just a cage inference

The decay of what?

The critic

He isn’t sure what the decay stands for. Perhaps that game design isn’t taken seriously, and the art form is regressing instead of advancing.

hypothesisSeveral readings at once

  • The critic’s reading. The Order was “tired of plodding forward,” its “membership was dwindling,” and “minds who had gone gray and dull” needed reinvigorating (audiolog_sky_combiners_4).
  • Aging. The Acting Director’s Warehouse is “the last I was able to create”; Lemur is still deciding what to build after 250 years; “Everything gets old at some point.”
  • Preserving games. The Order goes back to old games “as best we can” (audiolog_u_games_inspirations); it has a Preservation Crew, a motto (“just slow the rot. Beat it back. Preserve,” audiolog_u_decay_1) and the discovery that “fresh observers help with preservation.” In our world, the Video Game History Foundation and the Software Preservation Network found in 2023 that 87% of classic games released in the US are no longer commercially available. A Warehouse lives as long as someone plays it for the first time; so does a game.

As for the critic’s comparison with Death Stranding, an apocalypse driven by the power that ends up in one individual’s hands, the Acting Director spells it out almost literally: “Someone was feeling bad today, so he set off a bomb or unleashed a disease just to see a million souls squirm and die” (audiolog_sky_lifeboat_societies_1).

The critic, in a follow-up

The fresh-eyes reading of the decay (above), he concedes, seems closer to the truth than his own, though perhaps it is a blend: the Order needs fresh observers who can still experience the game in that more childlike state. He adds something he hadn’t mentioned: the holographic blocks peeking through the world are an obvious nod to aesthetic deception. As players move into the role of designers, they are less easily “tricked” by the aesthetic trappings and start seeing the game underneath. And perhaps his first reading held a grain of truth after all: the hope may be that if the art of game design advanced, the Order’s members could enjoy games in a more straightforward way again.

The blend holds, and the third ending makes the critic’s image literal inference Its art room hands the Visitor “a level with unfinished visuals. Play it first to understand it. Then we’ll make it look better” (judge_environment_art_background_play_first): the Visitor plays the bare blocks, then dresses them in shores, pillars and an ocean floor. The decay runs that lesson backwards: it strips the skin and shows the grid. The judge says plainly what the skin is for: art that conforms to the grid and helps “readability and believability” (judge_environment_art_grid_correct). The trappings are a deception, but an honest one, made to be believed by a player who has not yet learned to see through them. And the critic’s hope is the Order’s own. The Tetratetrad is “too dense, too hard to follow” for a newcomer, but “if they’d done a good ten or twenty other Warehouses first, they could work their way up to it” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_3): a design art advanced enough to surprise initiated minds as well. The decay, then, is two things at once: the price of seeing through, and the measure of how far the art still has to go.

Passing the torch

The critic

As with Kojima, there’s a strong sense here of passing the torch: holograms, meta-discussion, an ending that is a design course.

The motif starts with the game’s first line. The Queen prays before the relics of the one whose cause she inherited: “I am your ardent disciple, heir to your charge” (coda). The Director offers: “appoint a successor if you’re tired.” Reynard is grooming Ratleigh “to assume my duties.” The Acting Director calls her Warehouse “the last I was able to create, and the first I will soon pass over to you” (audiolog_sky_invocation). The third ending teaches “crossing the bridge from player to designer”; the credits say “(Thanks, Chat!)” and carry the dedication to Zach Polansky, an heir who is gone (chapter XI). And the epigraph’s poem is about Ulysses leaving the sceptre to his son (chapter X).

The critic admits the motif made him worry about Blow himself. For the record, Blow says he is still making games and does not “plan to quit anytime soon”; the next one already exists in a form a friend has played, and he hopes to make it “in less than 10 years” (livestream with Edmund McMillen). The torch in the game is passed to the player, not dropped by the author.

Recordings beside their subjects

The critic

The audio logs sit next to the puzzles they’re about, which is why they probably won’t be gathered into a menu. Compression and bits by “Beam Computation”; cellular automata in the mirrorable-mirror levels with buttons, where each press is a step of the automaton; the Dao in the eastern part of the upper tier, with its oriental architecture; predictability by “Goblin Turning,” where goblins try to predict the player; and the log about returning “to a childlike state” beside the single puzzle where every hero but the Bard is sacrificed to get the Druid to the one exit, which he takes for a nod to Lemmings. All this placement reminds him of a museum, the way (as he paraphrases it) Kojima saw games: an audio guide, frames, the order of the rooms.

The files confirm what they can. The cement recording is literally called audiolog_u_wizard_mirror_worlds_connect and is about the Wizard and the Mirror; the critic hears it beside a puzzle with the Wizard and mirrors. The recording on predictability (“If the world were completely predictable... If the world were too random and chaotic,” audiolog_sky_play_of_form_3) lies, he says, in Goblin Turning, and that zone’s internal ID is area_s_startle_reflex: the startle reflex, the body’s reaction to the unexpected. The recording itself is a debate over whether surprise is “a mere biological function” inference The Acting Director explains the principle of placement out loud (chapter IX), and one of the endgame tasks confirms that the Overworld’s regions have topics: “Imagine that the entry to this Level is located in a part of the Overworld whose topic is: stacks of mixed-up things” (sign_use_stack).

And the game’s vocabulary is a museum’s. Puzzles, the Acting Director says, “draw frames around specific instances of unfolding,” and “many instances, well-displayed together, fill out a rich, multidimensional image in our minds” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_1a); a significant portion of the Order’s Art is to “work out how to frame the ideas,” because “if everyone is just bored, or confused, they don’t see” (audiolog_sky_play_of_form_2). For the Director, “it just wasn’t good enough to curate a single beautiful world” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_2), and the revisions hall is laid out like an exhibition: stands, captions, a recording beside each version of a level.

The level list backs the critic up more than he knew code The designers named a whole family of levels, most of them built on mirrors, after the theory of computation: busy_beaver (the Turing-machine problem) and hanoi_3 in Mirrors as Obstacles on the lower tier; bitstring_small, mirror_clocks and iodine_clock_v2 (after the chemical clock reaction) in Heroes and Mirrors on the ground; binary_maze in Heroes and Light; and the four logic gates of Beam Computation. The automata he sensed are in the designers’ own vocabulary.

hypothesisLemonade and “Life”

The zone area_u_lemonade (“Mirrorable Mirror Medley,” on the same lower tier as the recording about “Life”) is named after its own level, lemonade, and that has a programmer’s punchline: “When life gives you lemons, make lemonade,” and “Life” is the name of Conway’s automaton that the Director leaves with Leamington. The zone’s second lemonade, lemonade_tng_stages_towers_no_float, adds a wink if tng means what it usually does, “the next generation”: in Life, every step of the automaton is a generation. And a nod to Lemmings beside the words about “nuggets of gold” from old games would be just such a nugget. None of this can be checked against the files.

Where the critic is simply right

The game doesn’t have an answer for everything.

  • The heroes’ storylines are dropped. After a certain point the characters barely speak, so much so that a line in the middle of a puzzle lands “almost like a jump scare,” and many threads are left hanging (chapter II, chapter XIII). Blow’s reason is real: dialogue running over a puzzle “drowns out the puzzle’s ability to speak,” so the lines stay short, “little nibbles,” and he accepts that “lots of people won’t” follow them (livestream with Edmund McMillen). A reason is not a resolution, though. Silence after a finished arc is one thing; silence in the middle of a promised story is another, and the Druid’s mission, Valens’s pardon and Aiah’s nature are all left mid-sentence.
  • Few puzzles bring three or four worlds together. The fan map counts 6 and 2 puzzles, out of 920, in the zones “Three at Once” and “Four Together.” The revisions hall even shows why: a level with ingredients from three worlds was cut down to two, “and that’s simpler too” (audiolog_di_across_the_fence_vtrader_m_p). In fairness, the count is a little higher than the zones suggest: the first ending adds four rooms for triples and one for all four worlds, the last level of the second ending gathers every world’s heroes, and the Overworld’s key routes are themselves four-world machines, like the ruby lift and the three-object plug in the hub shaft (chapter VIII). But dedicated puzzles for three and four worlds are rare, and the critic is right to want more.
  • Promesst’s scale isn’t kept. The critic expected more large levels in the spirit of Promesst; a stand in the revisions hall says the beam levels were deliberately tightened: “Final design. Clearer puzzles, cleaner wall shapes. Streamlined” (sign_design_promesst_3).

The critic dreams of an experiment: give the same four games to two teams and compare the cement each one chooses. In the Order this is routine: a world dropped from the Tetratetrad can go into “a different combiner” (audiolog_u_tetratetrad_troubles_1). And for the argument itself, the game has a line that sounds addressed to him. Having shown how the Wizard might meet the Mirror, the Director asks: “Would you have done it differently? But really think it through, because it’s easy just to say yes there, ‘Of course, my version would be better!’ Sometimes you’d be right. But you must always really think it through” (audiolog_u_wizard_mirror_worlds_connect). The critic ends in much the same place: it’s a very good game, and criticism is his way of meeting it where it is.

XIII

Open questions

What the game hints at and leaves open: no line, director’s note, sign or interview checked for this codex settles these. Next to each is what is known, which is sometimes more than it seems.

  1. Who is Aiah? The Druid knows legends of “her kind” that, according to a note, are kept secret because they are disturbing. She floats over water and hangs in the air above the goblins (by the rules, only while they strike her, which may also explain why the Druid “couldn’t do anything”), everyone around her starts confessing, and only Tif knows her name; a note says outright that the game never shows how Tif learned it. And once the Druid lets something slip: he calls her a “godsend” and, per the note, realizes he is “a bit too on the nose” (chapter II). One source of the name lies in plain sight: Reynard’s papers. Tif read them (“little notes tell you a lot about people, you know? Reading between the lines”), and his commission called for a Priestess hypothesis
  2. Who taught Reynard transmutation, and how did he gather goblins wherever he needed them? The Druid is amazed that a stranger mastered a method that “normally requires years of training.” According to a note, Reynard’s “special transport” was enabled by the Acting Director; whether the Order helped with anything else is never said, though the timing points that way (chapter II).
  3. Why doesn’t the description of the crystal maker that the Bard heard match? The Bard was hunting this man before she ever met the heroes, so her information doesn’t come from them. Did Reynard travel in disguise, like the stranger in the tavern, or was someone else making crystals in the East?
  4. What exactly did Valens do? The higher-ups “pretend to hate” him, the rank and file “respect what I did,” and the punishment goes by “a neutral term,” but the deed itself is never named (one reading is in chapter II). Nor do we learn whether the King will ever lift the binding.
  5. Who laid the interdicts and hid the chests? The Guild would have warned its sailors; the Surveyor thinks they are forgotten tests, “newly uncovered”; Borraidus suspects they guard treasure, and says storms have been gradually unearthing the chests; Janaila sees in the finds “remnants of an unknown people.” (The golden platform’s “pulse,” at least, is no longer a mystery: chapter I.)
  6. Who is the Surveyor really? The Guild knows something “significant” about her and held it back for a long time; the notes say she “infiltrated the Eastern Reaches under unknown circumstances” and is “not permitted” to say more; and Vedessud “did, actually” imagine the Warehouse in advance. Whom does she serve, and does the Farguild exist? She isn’t the only insider: every world has someone beside the heroes who knows more about the Warehouse than they say (chapter X).
  7. What happened during the Great Shutdown? Humans once learned to build beam producers from the dragons, then lost contact with them and even the memory of them. Why were the mines abandoned “in such a panic,” why was the rod buried, and what happened in the “times” the dragons say they hold no grudge about (chapter III)?
  8. How did the Queen get into the Warehouse? The Acting Director asks this herself: “Then by what method did she get here? Could matter a lot.” Blow leaves it open too: she “somehow ended up here, and there’s a question of how that happened.” A hole left by the decay, an unnamed deal, or the first step of the plan Reynard would rehearse two years later? She isn’t the only uninvited guest: Valens’s old teacher also turns up in the Warehouse, and asked how he got in, says: “I just ... came in!” And the Queen’s prayer rhymes with Reynard’s, which the Order once answered (chapter VIII).
  9. Whose cause did the Queen inherit, and what is “the Maw”? Her world appears only in the prologue and the coda: relics “for which we have searched so long,” a raid, a traitor with a bow, and a road that may lead “only into the Maw.” Who shot is never said. Arriving in the Warehouse, she calls two men by name, “Jackays! Conroe!”, before any loyal soldier; Jackays is the one who cries “Seize that traitor!”, and Conroe is never heard of again hypothesis
  10. Why did Leamington’s doorstead close? An accident, the decay (which the Order itself uses to explain uninvited guests), or Quigley shutting the door “with cold hands”? Both uninvited arrivals begin at a betrayal, real or feared: the Queen’s with a traitor’s arrow, Leamington’s with a door that may have been shut on purpose inference
  11. Will Leamington’s world become part of the Tetratetrad? Reynard studied it as a “lower-density substitution” and opened the way home for Leamington, yet in the endings, a couple of years later, Leamington is still with the Order and dreaming of a Warehouse of his own. Even in his solitude, home had become “a half-remembered dream,” and, a note says, he only half wanted to go back. The Order has a reason for the swap: the decay feeds on overly dense connections, and the Tetratetrad’s worlds were chosen for maximum density (chapter VII).
  12. How will the Director’s investigation end? What are the “Hook channels” really, can the decay be stopped, and will the Tetratetrad ever be finished? What is known about how the Warehouse works from the inside is gathered in chapter VII.
  13. Who is Lemur? The “First Founder,” who keeps “his spindly hands off of day-to-day matters” and is still deciding what to build 250 years after the founding. And what is the “arcane and auspicious” serial number? The best candidate is 69,105 from Zork: the number is even in the game’s data, on two gates by the Crossroads, but the game never calls it a serial number (chapter XI).
  14. What does “D(3,1,1)+MCu111” on the entrance sign mean? A possible reading is in chapter XI, but nothing confirms it.
  15. What does the Dynamic Brigade build, and what does the departed colony write? The Brigades are “firewalled” from each other, and the colony, “a low-density restart,” sends “only rare and cryptic communications.” If the static brigade makes puzzles with intended solutions, the dynamic one may make the other half of play (chapter XII).
  16. Does the Order know about Reynard’s “new hobby”? Every contact with an outside world speeds the decay, and the game’s last line belongs to a former jailer with the Order’s tools, looking over “the vast array of worlds available to mess with.” The Director, for his part, already keeps things from his own Preservation Crew (chapter VIII).
XIV

What this game is about

On the outside, Order of the Sinking Star is a “game design supercollider”: four puzzle games by other people, smashed together on one map. On the inside, it takes the humblest verb in video games, pushing a block across a grid, and follows it all the way up to cosmology. Movers hauling enchanted crystals, a caver sliding the lattice of a gem, a spirit steering a ship, an Order that travels between worlds by swapping their boundaries: all of them practice one art at different scales, applying rules to state and watching what blooms. The designers choose the rules; nobody chooses what the rules contain. A Wizard meeting a Mirror needed cement that someone had to pour, and once poured it could not be bent to fit a scene. What it held went beyond anything the four games’ authors had imagined, just as two worlds that never met drew the same infinite shape and named it twice, Mandelbrot and Clayson. In the Acting Director’s words, “Reality is always combining the elements we already have and producing things never before seen.”

The people who built the Warehouse outlived their planet because they refused to let the world become meaningless, and they made watching the Universe unfold into a spiritual practice. Their highest reward is the moment someone solves a puzzle and is surprised. But the art carries its own poison. “Density is good,” says Blow’s slide, and the Order lives the maxim to its end: to surprise ever stronger minds it weaves ever denser webs of rules, and density is exactly what rots its spaces, Leviathan’s scales packed so close “that no air can come between them.” What slows the rot is a first look, anyone’s first visit to a place; so the Order that forbids itself to speak to a Visitor cannot get enough of them. Its cosmos runs on newcomers, as every game does. And the game hopes, as its sharpest critic does, for an art of design good enough to surprise even those who have learned to see through it.

The central storylines turn a piece on the board into the hand that moves the pieces. The Trader, who spent his life gluing his shattered glass back together, finally stands whole and will learn to make mirrors. Leamington, who came uninvited through a door that closed behind him, becomes the Warehouse’s keeper. Reynard, whose despairing prayer was once answered by a woman in multicolored light, answers a doomed Queen’s. And the Queen, after living other people’s trials, hundreds of hours that fit into ten seconds of her own time, ends by rebuilding the climb she made and leaves with Leamington’s rank: Sokoban, a warehouse keeper, the very word that names the genre. The last rank is not a crown. It is a set of keys and a duty of upkeep.

At every rung the game asks what a maker owes the creatures in the maze, and two voices reply. The first is Miltonic and dryly practical: “We hold a high position. We must not use it for cruelty.” Cut the noise, give the player a fair shot, and trust that what looks like chaos from inside the cage may be a message you don’t yet have the key to. The second voice is less comfortable, and the game lets it speak. Its makers are tired, proud and fallible: a Founder who finds every plan too small, an Acting Director who proposed the leap that would rot her Order and tells the story with herself left out, a Director who keeps things from his own colleagues, a judge who admits he may not have found every hole, a former jailer who turned people into puzzle pieces and has found a “new hobby” in other people’s worlds. The course’s fairness covers the player; nobody in the Order speaks for the crystals. Even the title’s Ulysses is a hero and a deserter at once. The game honors the voyage and does not hide the bill.

Its best image of design is buried in the code. A beam sent straight up with nothing to stop it runs out of the world, and whoever rides it leaves the map, beyond the reach of anything but undo; the same beam, caught at the top by a ring of four mirrors, becomes the lift to the sky. The rules are the same in both cases, because Blow refuses to bend them. Between a fall into infinity and an ascent stands one piece, placed with care. Every star on the map holds an idea, and the Order’s own star really is sinking. But in “Ulysses” a sinking star is not something to mourn. It is something to follow, with the mirrors in place.

The game also carries its own history: the memory of Zach Polansky, and a design question no one can put to him now; a self-portrait of ten years of work, with its discarded worlds and its puzzles sent to Cold Storage; a bow to the designers whose four free puzzle games became its worlds; and an argument with its critics that it starts before they do, wins in places and loses in others.

To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield.

The last line of “Ulysses.” In the game it, too, stands on a sign (sign_strive).

The skill of guessing the designer’s intent from a level’s geometry. In the third ending, the bridge “from player to designer”; on its price, see chapter XII.
SwordbindingSwordbound
A punishment for disgraced swordsmen: you can’t swing anything at anyone. Energy squeezed through it gives enormous pushing strength.
The Crawler
A giant overland transport across the Badlands, with a passenger terminal.
Transmutation
The druids’ technique: “a sort of ... descent through corporeal memory” to a stony state and back. The public is told it’s an inborn ability.
Beamcaster
A machine that, once every gem socket in the room is filled, emits a colored beam with a property.
At infinityendpoint_b_is_at_infinity
The end of a beam that leaves the level with nothing to stop it. Whoever steps along it leaves the map; only undo brings them back.
Ruby lift
The Endpoint lift by the Crossroads that first carries the Visitor to the sky; a ring of four mirrors at the top keeps its beam from running to infinity.
Gemsuitshifter
A suit of bone, leather and sinew that beams can permeate; its shifter miniaturizes gems by sliding their lattice.
The Great Shutdown
The panicked closing of the mines in the world of Promesst; the reason is kept secret. Humans had once learned beam-making from the dragons, then lost contact with them.
Nidi
Spaces that dragons “extrude”; sometimes they coincide with human mines.
Displacerthe rod
An alabaster rod that moves an object through a wall to the next free tile.
Torc, prism
The torc turns the beams that hit you; the prism switches an emitter’s color.
Interdict
Marks that make a Guild ship lock its own controls on sight.
Scriers
Inserts in a ship’s control struts that “constantly watch for interdiction marks.”
Motive principle
“It’s what makes the stones go”: what keeps a skipping stone flying until it hits something.
FootsailorJackashore
A sailor who works ashore; “Jackashore” is a teasing nickname.
Deadwaters
A sea in the east, flat as polished onyx, that “eats” the energy of skipping stones.
Farguild
A presumed empire beyond the Deadwaters.
Conflux
The second “feature” of the islands in Borraidus’s hint. The word means a flowing together of rivers; judging by the order of his warnings, it is the giant whirlpool.
Mirrorwrights
The College of makers of magic mirrors, with its Chiefs, trials and apprentices.
“Keeping busy?”
The traditional greeting on the Mirror Isles, answered with the same words.
Doorstead
A portal that connects space itself; in Leamington’s world, common technology for a hundred years. The special one his group built uses hundreds of gears.
Clayson’s Field
What Leamington’s world calls the Mandelbrot set.
“Lavish expenditure”
Reynard’s signature phrase, by which the Order’s deals can be recognized across the worlds.
